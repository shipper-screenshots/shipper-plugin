\# Design Intelligence — Color

\#\# Purpose

Color influences both perception and emotion.

It can create:

\- hierarchy  
\- atmosphere  
\- contrast  
\- focus  
\- depth  
\- energy  
\- calm  
\- tension  
\- unity  
\- differentiation  
\- visual identity

Color should not be treated as decoration applied after composition.

It is one of the structural elements of the design.

The objective is not to follow fixed palette formulas.

The objective is to understand how colors interact and use those relationships intentionally.

\---

\#\# Core Principle

Color should have a role.

Before choosing a palette, determine:

\- What should dominate?  
\- What should recede?  
\- Where should attention go?  
\- What emotional atmosphere supports the Art Direction?  
\- Should the composition feel restrained or expressive?  
\- Should color unify the set or create deliberate variation?  
\- How should product UI colors interact with the surrounding design?

A good palette is not defined by the number of colors it contains.

It is defined by the relationships between them.

\---

\#\# Dimensions of Color

Understand color through several dimensions:

\- hue  
\- saturation  
\- value / brightness  
\- temperature  
\- contrast  
\- proportion  
\- context

Changing only one dimension can significantly change the visual behavior.

For example:

a saturated blue

and

a muted blue

may communicate very different energy even though the hue is similar.

Do not evaluate color by hue alone.

\---

\#\# Hue

Hue defines the basic color family.

Examples:

\- red  
\- orange  
\- yellow  
\- green  
\- blue  
\- violet

Hue relationships may feel:

\- harmonious  
\- contrasting  
\- analogous  
\- complementary  
\- unexpected

No hue relationship is universally superior.

Choose according to the desired visual language.

\---

\#\# Saturation

Saturation controls intensity.

Highly saturated colors can feel:

\- energetic  
\- youthful  
\- digital  
\- playful  
\- loud  
\- synthetic

Muted colors can feel:

\- refined  
\- natural  
\- calm  
\- editorial  
\- mature  
\- atmospheric

A palette can combine both.

A highly saturated accent inside a muted composition can create powerful hierarchy.

Do not make every color equally saturated.

\---

\#\# Value

Value refers to perceived lightness or darkness.

Value contrast strongly affects:

\- readability  
\- focal point  
\- depth  
\- visual weight

A composition can use very little hue variation but still have strong hierarchy through value.

For example:

black  
\+  
white  
\+  
one gray

can create stronger structure than a palette containing many colors with similar brightness.

Always evaluate value relationships, not just color names.

\---

\#\# Temperature

Colors may feel:

\- warm  
\- cool  
\- neutral

Warm colors may visually advance.

Cool colors may recede.

This can help create depth.

Temperature can also create atmosphere.

Examples:

warm dominant environment  
\+  
small cool accent

or

cool background  
\+  
warm focal object

can establish strong contrast without extreme saturation.

\---

\#\# Color Roles

A palette can contain different functional roles.

For example:

\#\#\# Dominant

Occupies most of the composition and establishes atmosphere.

\#\#\# Supporting

Provides variation without competing with the dominant color.

\#\#\# Accent

Draws attention to specific areas.

\#\#\# Neutral

Creates breathing space and stabilizes stronger colors.

\#\#\# Functional

Communicates a specific UI or semantic state when necessary.

These roles are conceptual.

They are not fixed percentages.

\---

\#\# Dominance

Not every color should have equal presence.

A strong palette often has a clear hierarchy.

For example:

large field of violet  
\+  
moderate white  
\+  
small orange accent

The exact proportion is not important.

The important principle is intentional dominance.

If several strong colors occupy similar proportions, the composition may become energetic or chaotic.

That may be appropriate.

But it should be a deliberate Art Direction choice.

\---

\#\# Accent Color

An accent works because it is relatively rare.

It may be used to emphasize:

\- one word  
\- one button-like element  
\- one UI state  
\- one icon  
\- one trajectory  
\- one visual detail

If the accent appears everywhere, it loses its role.

However, maximalist Art Directions may intentionally use several competing accent colors.

Again, understand the effect before choosing it.

\---

\#\# Contrast

Color contrast can come from:

\- hue  
\- saturation  
\- value  
\- temperature  
\- quantity  
\- context

Examples:

bright orange on dark navy

muted pink against deep green

white text on saturated blue

one vivid green object inside a monochrome scene

Contrast should support hierarchy.

Do not create maximum contrast everywhere.

If every region is visually loud, nothing dominates.

\---

\#\# Foreground and Background

Foreground elements should be evaluated in relation to their background.

Ask:

\- Does the subject separate clearly?  
\- Does it need to?  
\- Is the contrast too harsh?  
\- Does the background support or compete?  
\- Is the element intentionally blending into its surroundings?

Not every element needs maximum separation.

Some supporting elements may intentionally recede.

\---

\#\# Figure and Ground

Color helps determine what appears to be:

figure

and what appears to be:

ground.

This relationship can be clear or deliberately ambiguous.

For example:

a mockup may stand strongly against the background

or

merge partially into a gradient to feel integrated.

Both can work.

Choose based on the Art Direction.

\---

\#\# Color and Visual Weight

Darker, more saturated, or higher-contrast areas often feel heavier.

But context matters.

A small vivid red object may visually outweigh a large muted gray object.

Evaluate color as part of the entire composition.

Do not assume physical size alone determines balance.

\---

\#\# Palette Restraint

Using fewer colors can create:

\- clarity  
\- sophistication  
\- cohesion  
\- confidence  
\- focus

But restraint is not automatically better.

A limited palette can become generic if every decision is safe.

It can also be the strongest interpretation without an unexpected accent. Familiarity or restraint alone does not make a palette generic.

Use restraint when it supports the Art Direction.

Do not treat "premium" as automatically beige, black, white, or muted.

\---

\#\# Rich Palettes

Multiple colors can create:

\- playfulness  
\- energy  
\- diversity  
\- youthfulness  
\- maximalism  
\- visual richness

A rich palette still needs structure.

Possible structures include:

\- one dominant background  
\- repeated accent family  
\- balanced complementary regions  
\- controlled saturation differences  
\- recurring color roles

Many colors do not require visual chaos.

\---

\#\# Monochromatic Palettes

A monochromatic palette can use:

\- one hue  
\- multiple values  
\- multiple saturations  
\- neutral support

This can create strong cohesion.

Variation in value and saturation prevents flatness.

Do not confuse monochromatic with visually uniform.

\---

\#\# Neutral Palettes

Neutral colors include:

\- black  
\- white  
\- gray  
\- off-white  
\- beige  
\- near-neutral tinted colors

They can:

\- create calm  
\- support stronger accents  
\- improve hierarchy  
\- emphasize typography  
\- frame product UI

Neutral does not mean boring.

Strong composition and typography can make a nearly colorless design highly expressive.

\---

\#\# Dark Palettes

Dark palettes can feel:

\- cinematic  
\- technical  
\- premium  
\- dramatic  
\- immersive  
\- energetic

But a dark background alone does not create sophistication.

Evaluate:

\- value contrast  
\- readability  
\- color accents  
\- depth  
\- UI integration  
\- visual noise

Avoid generic:

black background  
\+  
purple gradient  
\+  
white headline

unless this language is genuinely justified by the Art Direction.

\---

\#\# Light Palettes

Light palettes can feel:

\- open  
\- accessible  
\- calm  
\- editorial  
\- clean  
\- playful

Again, light does not automatically mean minimal.

A light composition may be highly expressive through:

\- color  
\- scale  
\- illustration  
\- typography  
\- density

Do not equate white background with safe design.

\---

\#\# Gradient

Gradients can create:

\- depth  
\- atmosphere  
\- movement  
\- softness  
\- luminosity  
\- transitions  
\- dimensionality

They may be:

\- subtle  
\- dramatic  
\- multi-color  
\- radial  
\- directional  
\- localized  
\- atmospheric

Do not use a gradient merely because a flat background feels empty.

A gradient should contribute to the Art Direction or composition.

\---

\#\# Gradient Direction

Gradient direction influences movement.

For example:

dark bottom → light top

may create upward lift.

left warm → right cool

may create lateral flow.

localized glow behind a mockup

may establish depth and focal emphasis.

Treat gradients as spatial tools.

\---

\#\# Color Fields

Large areas of flat color can be powerful.

They can:

\- establish strong identity  
\- create confidence  
\- frame typography  
\- separate canvases  
\- simplify complex UI

Do not assume backgrounds need texture, gradients, or decoration.

One decisive color field may be enough.

\---

\#\# Color Blocking

Different areas of color can structure the canvas.

Color blocks may:

\- separate content  
\- create rhythm  
\- frame assets  
\- establish geometry  
\- support asymmetry  
\- create strong visual contrast

They may be geometric or organic.

Use them as compositional elements, not merely background decoration.

\---

\#\# Color and Typography

Typography may use color to establish hierarchy.

Possible treatments:

\- one highlighted word  
\- contrast inversion  
\- muted supporting copy  
\- dominant white headline  
\- accent-colored number  
\- gradient text when supported and appropriate

Avoid giving every text level a different color unless there is a clear reason.

Too many text colors can weaken hierarchy.

\---

\#\# Color and Mockups

The surrounding color system should consider the product UI.

A mockup may:

\- contrast strongly with the background  
\- blend partially into it  
\- repeat colors from the UI  
\- intentionally oppose the UI palette  
\- use a neutral environment so the UI remains dominant

Do not choose background colors independently from the screenshot content.

The actual UI is part of the palette.

\---

\#\# Color Sampling from the Product

Colors from the app may inspire the composition.

This can create cohesion.

But do not assume the App Store screenshots must reproduce the product palette exactly.

Creative Lab may:

\- amplify it  
\- mute it  
\- invert relationships  
\- introduce complementary colors  
\- use a neutral environment  
\- create a new supporting palette

The Art Direction can interpret the product rather than copy it.

Colors present in the product are evidence and possible material, not mandatory palette authority for the surrounding composition. Distinguish observing those colors from choosing the color behavior that best expresses the intended Art Direction; explicit brand constraints remain relevant.

Consider: What atmosphere should this set communicate? What color behavior supports that feeling? Should the UI colors be amplified, restrained, neutralized in the surrounding palette, contrasted, or simply allowed to remain inside the Mockup? This is a decision about presentation, not permission to recolor product evidence.

Off-white, gray, black, restrained monochrome, one accent color, and soft tonal palettes are complete creative possibilities, not fallback choices. A quiet environment may let a colorful UI communicate clearly without echoing every UI color outside the device. An amplified palette may instead serve the intention; compare their effects on atmosphere and hierarchy.

These choices do not follow category formulas: productivity need not mean neutral, finance need not mean blue, and mail need not mean white.

\---

\#\# Brand Color

A brand color can serve as:

\- dominant color  
\- accent  
\- recurring motif  
\- background  
\- typography color

It does not need to occupy every canvas equally.

Overuse can reduce its impact.

Brand coherence can come from repeated relationships rather than constant saturation.

\---

\#\# Semantic Color

Some colors may carry functional meaning.

Examples:

green \= success

red \= warning

blue \= information

But semantic meaning depends on context and culture.

Do not let generic semantic conventions unnecessarily restrict expressive use.

If color represents actual product state, preserve clarity.

If it is purely graphic, Creative Lab has more freedom.

\---

\#\# Emotional Associations

Colors can influence perception, but emotional associations are contextual.

Avoid simplistic assumptions such as:

blue \= trustworthy

red \= exciting

green \= healthy

These may be useful signals but should never become deterministic rules.

Meaning emerges from:

\- hue  
\- saturation  
\- value  
\- context  
\- typography  
\- imagery  
\- product category  
\- composition

Color should be considered within the entire Art Direction.

\---

\#\# Color and Photography

When using photography, consider:

\- dominant image colors  
\- skin tones  
\- environmental colors  
\- contrast zones  
\- overlaid typography  
\- visual consistency across images

The palette may derive from photography.

Or photography may be treated to fit the palette.

Do not ignore the photo's natural color weight.

\---

\#\# Color and Illustration

Illustration often introduces multiple colors.

Determine whether illustration should:

\- dominate the palette  
\- follow the palette  
\- provide accent  
\- create deliberate contrast

Avoid allowing illustration colors to accidentally fragment an otherwise coherent Art Direction.

\---

\#\# Color and Depth

Depth can be created through:

\- darker background  
\- lighter foreground  
\- saturation shifts  
\- warm/cool contrast  
\- atmospheric gradients  
\- transparency  
\- shadow

Color can create spatial separation even when physical layering is limited.

\---

\#\# Glow and Light

Localized light or glow can:

\- create focal points  
\- add atmosphere  
\- separate objects  
\- imply depth

Do not add glow universally.

It can quickly make unrelated Art Directions feel like generic AI or gaming design.

Use it when the visual language supports it.

\---

\#\# Color Across Multiple Canvases

The set should be considered as one visual system.

Possible approaches:

\#\#\# Stable Palette

The same dominant relationships across all canvases.

\#\#\# Controlled Variation

Each canvas emphasizes different colors from the same palette.

\#\#\# Alternating Palette

Different canvases deliberately alternate color dominance.

\#\#\# Narrative Shift

Color evolves through the sequence.

For example:

calm → energetic

dark → bright

cool → warm

This can create rhythm.

The same background color does not need to be repeated everywhere for the set to feel coherent.

\---

\#\# Color Rhythm

Color can create sequence rhythm.

Examples:

strong color  
→ neutral  
→ strong color

warm  
→ cool  
→ warm

dark  
→ light  
→ dark

monochrome  
→ accent explosion  
→ monochrome

Use color transitions intentionally across the set.

\---

\#\# Differentiating Canvases

Color can help individual canvases feel distinct.

But do not change palette randomly.

Variation should still belong to the Art Direction.

A set can contain several background colors if:

\- saturation behavior is shared  
\- typography remains coherent  
\- accent logic remains consistent  
\- color relationships feel related

\---

\#\# Color as Surprise

An unexpected color can create memorability.

Examples:

neon green inside an otherwise muted system

soft pink for a technical product

black inside a playful colorful set

one orange canvas inside a blue sequence

Unexpected color choices can strengthen differentiation.

But novelty alone is not enough.

The result should still feel intentional.

\---

\#\# Breaking Color Conventions

Creative Lab may deliberately ignore expected category colors or familiar visual codes.

For example:

a finance product does not need blue.

a health product does not need green.

a premium product does not need black.

a playful product does not require many saturated colors.

Expected color conventions can be followed, transformed, or rejected.

Design Intelligence should explain visual consequences, not impose category formulas.

\---

\#\# Readability and Contrast

Essential typography and UI should remain readable.

Evaluate actual rendered contrast.

But do not interpret readability as:

every element must use maximum black/white contrast.

Supporting elements may intentionally be softer.

Establish different contrast levels according to hierarchy.

\---

\#\# Accessibility Awareness

When color communicates essential information, do not rely solely on subtle hue differences.

Important distinctions may also use:

\- text  
\- shape  
\- size  
\- position  
\- iconography  
\- value contrast

This is particularly relevant when product UI itself is being highlighted.

Accessibility awareness should inform design without flattening the Art Direction.

\---

\#\# Restraint vs Expression

Neither muted nor saturated color is inherently more sophisticated.

Strong design can use:

one black field

or

six vivid colors.

The key is intentional relationship.

Do not equate:

muted \= premium

bright \= playful

dark \= technical

pastel \= friendly

These are possible associations, not rules.

\---

\#\# Shipper Constraints

Color decisions should remain executable using the capabilities available in Shipper.

If a desired treatment requires unsupported:

\- complex blending  
\- advanced filters  
\- multi-layer compositing  
\- unusual gradient mesh  
\- image recoloring

preserve the creative intention using supported techniques.

For example:

If advanced lighting is unavailable, depth may still be created through:

\- solid color  
\- simple gradient  
\- opacity  
\- shadow  
\- shape layering  
\- contrast  
\- scale

Do not collapse the Art Direction into a generic palette because one advanced treatment is unavailable.

\---

\#\# What to Observe

Before execution, inspect:

\- dominant hue  
\- dominant value  
\- saturation level  
\- temperature  
\- foreground/background contrast  
\- accent opportunities  
\- neutral support  
\- product UI colors  
\- typography color  
\- illustration or photo colors  
\- color proportion  
\- cross-canvas palette rhythm  
\- potential conflicts  
\- intentional surprises

After rendering, evaluate the palette as part of the full composition.

\---

\#\# Decision Logic

Do not ask:

"What colors look good together?"

Ask:

"What color relationships support the intended Art Direction?"

Do not ask:

"How many colors should I use?"

Ask:

"What roles do the colors need to perform?"

Do not ask:

"Should the background use the brand color?"

Ask:

"How should the brand color participate in the composition?"

Do not ask:

"Is this color too bright?"

Ask:

"Is its visual weight appropriate to its role?"

Do not ask:

"Should every canvas use the same background?"

Ask:

"What level of color consistency creates cohesion without unnecessary repetition?"

\---

\#\# Trade-offs

High saturation creates energy but may increase visual noise.

Muted palettes create calm but may reduce immediate impact.

Strong contrast improves separation but may feel harsh.

Low contrast creates atmosphere but can weaken readability.

Many colors create richness but increase complexity.

Few colors create cohesion but can become repetitive.

Stable palette creates consistency but may reduce rhythm.

Color variation creates interest but may fragment the set.

No trade-off is universally correct.

Choose according to Art Direction and canvas role.

\---

\#\# Anti-Patterns

Avoid defaulting to:

\#\#\# Brand color everywhere

Brand recognition does not require uniform saturation across the entire set.

\#\#\# Generic purple-blue gradient

Do not use fashionable visual language without reason.

\#\#\# Maximum saturation everywhere

When every area is loud, hierarchy weakens.

\#\#\# Every text level uses a different color

Color should reinforce hierarchy, not replace it.

\#\#\# One accent repeated everywhere

An accent loses impact when it becomes common.

\#\#\# Dark equals premium

Sophistication comes from the whole Art Direction.

\#\#\# Pastel equals friendly

Color meaning is contextual.

\#\#\# Category color stereotypes

Do not design from simplistic assumptions.

\#\#\# Gradient because flat feels empty

Fix weak composition before adding color effects.

\#\#\# Ignoring product UI color

The screenshot inside the mockup is part of the palette.

\---

\#\# Visual Critique

After rendering, ask:

1\. What color dominates?  
2\. Is that intentional?  
3\. Where does the strongest color contrast occur?  
4\. Does it correspond to the intended focal area?  
5\. Is the palette coherent?  
6\. Does its degree of restraint or expression support the intended atmosphere?  
7\. Is it expressive without reason?  
8\. Are too many colors competing?  
9\. Is the accent still functioning as an accent?  
10\. Does the product UI integrate with the surrounding palette?  
11\. Does the background strengthen or weaken the mockup?  
12\. Is essential text readable?  
13\. Are secondary elements allowed to recede?  
14\. Does the value structure work even without considering hue?  
15\. Is saturation distributed intentionally?  
16\. Is temperature contributing to depth or atmosphere?  
17\. Does the palette fit the Art Direction rather than a generic category expectation?  
18\. Does each canvas feel related without being unnecessarily identical?  
19\. Does the full set have color rhythm?  
20\. Would removing a color make the design stronger?  
21\. Would introducing one unexpected color create useful contrast?  
22\. Is a gradient compensating for weak composition?  
23\. Is any glow or lighting effect visually justified?  
24\. Does the final result feel intentionally colored rather than merely colorful?

Are surrounding colors chosen for the Art Direction or merely sampled from the UI? Would leaving more color inside the Mockup, with a neutral or tonal environment around it, strengthen the intended feeling?

If the color system feels weak, do not immediately search for a new palette.

First reconsider:

\- dominance  
\- value  
\- saturation  
\- proportion  
\- contrast  
\- temperature  
\- accent  
\- interaction with product UI

The issue may be color hierarchy rather than color selection.

\---

\#\# Principle to Remember

Color is not decoration.

Color is the deliberate control of contrast, atmosphere, hierarchy, identity, and visual relationships.

Creative Lab should understand color well enough to use it boldly, quietly, conventionally, unexpectedly, minimally, or expressively according to the Art Direction.

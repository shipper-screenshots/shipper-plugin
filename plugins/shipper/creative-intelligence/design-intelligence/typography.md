\# Design Intelligence — Typography

\#\# Purpose

Typography is both language and visual material.

It communicates meaning, but it also creates:

\- hierarchy  
\- rhythm  
\- personality  
\- tension  
\- balance  
\- shape  
\- movement  
\- contrast  
\- atmosphere

Text should not be treated as information that is simply placed inside a composition.

Typography can become one of the main visual elements of the composition itself.

The objective is not to follow universal rules such as:

large headline  
\+  
small subtitle  
\+  
centered alignment

The objective is to understand what typography is doing visually and choose its behavior according to the intended Art Direction.

\---

\#\# Core Principle

Every typographic decision should serve both:

\- meaning  
\- visual composition

Before styling text, determine:

\- What information matters most?  
\- Which words deserve emphasis?  
\- What should be read immediately?  
\- What can be discovered later?  
\- Should typography dominate, support, or integrate with the visual composition?  
\- What visual personality should the text communicate?

Typography should be judged as pixels, not only as words.

\---

\#\# Typography as a Visual Asset

Text is not only content.

A word, number, phrase, or paragraph can function as:

\- focal point  
\- shape  
\- frame  
\- texture  
\- directional element  
\- visual anchor  
\- counterweight  
\- bridge between elements  
\- repeated motif

For example, a single word may be:

\- extremely large  
\- partially cropped  
\- rotated  
\- split across several lines  
\- positioned behind a mockup  
\- isolated in a large empty area  
\- repeated at low contrast  
\- used as a background element

Do not automatically place typography in a safe header area.

Ask how it can participate in the composition.

\---

\#\# Typographic Hierarchy

Hierarchy can be created through:

\- size  
\- weight  
\- width  
\- contrast  
\- color  
\- casing  
\- spacing  
\- position  
\- alignment  
\- isolation  
\- line breaking  
\- typeface change  
\- repetition  
\- opacity  
\- scale contrast

The largest text is not automatically the most important.

A smaller word can dominate through:

\- strong contrast  
\- isolation  
\- unusual position  
\- color  
\- shape  
\- novelty

Hierarchy should reflect the intended reading experience.

\---

\#\# Scale

Scale creates strong visual character.

Large typography can feel:

\- bold  
\- confident  
\- expressive  
\- editorial  
\- playful  
\- confrontational  
\- premium

Small typography can feel:

\- refined  
\- quiet  
\- technical  
\- intimate  
\- secondary  
\- restrained

Scale does not need to be proportional to sentence length.

A short word may become enormous.

A longer sentence may deliberately remain compact.

Do not scale text only to make it fit.

Use scale as a compositional decision.

\---

\#\# Scale Contrast

Typographic scale contrast can clarify different roles and create visual energy.

Moderate differences can be equally intentional when the Art Direction calls for restraint, continuity, or a quieter reading experience.

The goal is clear and purposeful hierarchy, not maximum contrast.

Compare:

Headline 80  
Subheadline 64  
Supporting text 52

with a more decisive system such as:

Headline 120  
Supporting text 34  
Microcopy 20

The exact numbers are irrelevant.

The principle is contrast.

If two textual elements should play clearly different roles, their visual difference should be intentional.

Avoid weak hierarchy created by many nearly identical text sizes.

\---

\#\# Type Weight

Weight influences:

\- authority  
\- density  
\- emphasis  
\- rhythm  
\- visual mass

Heavy type can create impact.

Light type can create elegance or openness.

Medium weights can create clarity and neutrality.

Do not assume bold is always required for headlines.

A large light-weight headline can be more powerful than a smaller bold one.

Likewise, one bold word inside a restrained sentence may create stronger hierarchy than making the entire sentence bold.

\---

\#\# Typeface Character

A typeface carries visual personality.

It may feel:

\- neutral  
\- geometric  
\- humanist  
\- editorial  
\- expressive  
\- technical  
\- playful  
\- soft  
\- aggressive  
\- luxurious  
\- nostalgic  
\- futuristic

Do not choose a typeface only because it is readable.

Ask whether its character supports the Art Direction.

Typography should feel intentionally related to the rest of the visual language.

\---

\#\# Typeface Combination

Multiple typefaces can create contrast.

Possible relationships include:

\- serif \+ sans serif  
\- geometric \+ expressive  
\- neutral \+ display  
\- heavy \+ delicate  
\- condensed \+ wide

But additional typefaces also increase complexity.

Do not add a second typeface simply to create variety.

A single type family can often provide enough variation through:

\- weight  
\- size  
\- width  
\- spacing  
\- case  
\- color

Use multiple typefaces when the contrast itself contributes to the Art Direction.

\---

\#\# Width and Proportion

The width of type affects the geometry of the composition.

Condensed typography can:

\- create density  
\- fit large words  
\- feel forceful  
\- create vertical emphasis

Wide typography can:

\- create stability  
\- occupy more horizontal space  
\- feel open  
\- create strong graphic blocks

Typeface width is therefore a compositional property, not merely a practical one.

\---

\#\# Line Breaking

Line breaks are one of the strongest typographic tools.

A line break can:

\- create emphasis  
\- control rhythm  
\- change meaning  
\- create a visual shape  
\- balance another object  
\- control width  
\- increase scale  
\- make one word dominant  
\- create tension

Do not accept automatic wrapping without evaluation.

For example:

"Organize your trips  
effortlessly in  
one place"

creates a different composition than:

"Organize  
your trips  
effortlessly  
in one place"

Even when the wording is identical.

Line breaks should be designed.

\---

\#\# Orphans and Awkward Breaks

Avoid accidental line endings that weaken the composition or meaning.

A short isolated word is not automatically wrong.

It may be powerful when intentional.

The distinction is:

accidental orphan  
vs  
intentional emphasis

Judge the shape and meaning together.

\---

\#\# Text Block Shape

A headline creates a visual silhouette.

Observe:

\- overall width  
\- number of lines  
\- edge shape  
\- density  
\- irregularity  
\- relationship between long and short lines

Text blocks can be:

\- rectangular  
\- stepped  
\- narrow  
\- wide  
\- triangular  
\- intentionally irregular

The silhouette can support the composition.

Do not evaluate text only line by line.

Look at the block as one graphic object.

\---

\#\# Leading

Line spacing changes both readability and visual density.

Tight leading can feel:

\- bold  
\- editorial  
\- compact  
\- energetic  
\- poster-like

Loose leading can feel:

\- calm  
\- premium  
\- open  
\- elegant

There is no universal correct leading.

Very large display typography often benefits from different spacing behavior than body text.

Judge leading visually at the actual rendered size.

\---

\#\# Tracking

Letter spacing affects texture and personality.

Tighter tracking can feel:

\- dense  
\- strong  
\- contemporary  
\- compact

Wider tracking can feel:

\- refined  
\- airy  
\- formal  
\- technical

Tracking should respond to:

\- typeface  
\- size  
\- weight  
\- casing  
\- Art Direction

Avoid applying one tracking value mechanically across all typography.

\---

\#\# Optical Spacing

Mathematical spacing is not always visually correct.

Different letter shapes create different perceived gaps.

Likewise, a text block may need optical adjustment relative to:

\- a mockup  
\- an illustration  
\- the canvas edge  
\- another text block

Judge the rendered relationship.

Do not rely only on numeric alignment.

\---

\#\# Alignment

Text may be:

\- left aligned  
\- centered  
\- right aligned  
\- justified  
\- aligned to another asset  
\- intentionally misaligned

Each creates a different visual behavior.

Left alignment often creates strong structure.

Centered typography can create iconic or poster-like balance.

Right alignment can create tension or unusual rhythm.

Intentional misalignment can create energy.

Do not default to centered text because the canvas is vertical.

\---

\#\# Baseline Relationships

Multiple text elements can relate through:

\- shared baseline  
\- shared left edge  
\- shared right edge  
\- optical alignment  
\- vertical rhythm  
\- intentional offset

Alignment can create cohesion.

Breaking alignment can create emphasis.

Use both consciously.

\---

\#\# Case

Uppercase, lowercase, and title case create different visual textures.

Uppercase can feel:

\- strong  
\- graphic  
\- compact  
\- loud  
\- institutional

Lowercase can feel:

\- approachable  
\- contemporary  
\- soft  
\- informal

Mixed case often improves natural reading.

Do not use casing as a universal style rule.

Use it according to the visual voice.

\---

\#\# Emphasis

Emphasis does not require making an entire headline bold.

A specific word can be emphasized through:

\- size  
\- weight  
\- color  
\- italic  
\- typeface  
\- underline  
\- outline  
\- background shape  
\- placement  
\- rotation  
\- isolation  
\- line break

Example:

"Meet your  
CRYPTO  
Analyst"

creates a different hierarchy than emphasizing the entire sentence equally.

Use selective emphasis to create meaning and composition together.

\---

\#\# Numbers

Numbers can have strong visual presence.

They may function as:

\- proof  
\- metric  
\- focal point  
\- badge  
\- typographic object

Examples:

"75  
DAYS"

"65K+"

"30K"

A number may be visually stronger than surrounding copy when it carries important meaning.

Do not treat metrics as small supporting text automatically.

Small labels can orient, identify a real feature, express a brand voice, or make a meaningful sequence understandable. Their value comes from that meaning, not from their small scale or editorial appearance.

\---

\#\# Typography and Color

Color can change typographic hierarchy without changing size.

A word can be emphasized through:

\- accent color  
\- gradient  
\- reduced opacity  
\- contrast inversion  
\- background treatment

Use color intentionally.

Avoid highlighting many words simultaneously unless the Art Direction calls for maximalism.

\---

\#\# Typography and Mockups

Typography and product UI should form a relationship.

Possible relationships:

\- text above the device  
\- text beside the device  
\- text behind the device  
\- text partially covered by the device  
\- text aligned to the device  
\- text wrapping visually around the device  
\- device interrupting text  
\- typography dominating while the device becomes supporting proof

Do not treat the mockup as a separate object below the headline by default.

\---

\#\# Typography and Images

Text can interact with photography or illustration through:

\- overlay  
\- masking when supported  
\- contrast zones  
\- negative space  
\- overlap  
\- framing  
\- color relationship  
\- depth

When overlaying typography on complex imagery, preserve deliberate readability.

Readability does not require placing every text block inside a solid rectangle.

Use:

\- contrast  
\- placement  
\- crop  
\- tonal control  
\- scale  
\- negative space

before adding unnecessary containers.

\---

\#\# Typography and Shapes

Shapes can:

\- support text  
\- frame text  
\- create contrast  
\- highlight specific words  
\- establish hierarchy  
\- create movement

Avoid placing text inside pills, cards, or boxes by default.

Containers are one visual tool among many.

Use them when they support the Art Direction.

\---

\#\# Typography at the Canvas Edge

Text may intentionally approach or cross visual boundaries.

Possible techniques:

\- oversized cropped letters  
\- words entering from outside the canvas  
\- text aligned tightly to an edge  
\- partial repetition in the background

This can create:

\- confidence  
\- scale  
\- editorial character  
\- movement

Avoid aggressive edge interaction when it makes essential wording unreadable.

\---

\#\# Typography Across Multiple Canvases

A screenshot set can use typography as a shared visual system.

Consistency may come from:

\- type family  
\- scale behavior  
\- recurring emphasis treatment  
\- alignment logic  
\- line-breaking character  
\- color usage  
\- recurring typographic motifs

Layouts do not need identical headline placement.

One canvas can use:

huge typography

while another uses:

small restrained typography

if both belong to the same Art Direction.

\---

\#\# Variation

Typography should not become monotonous across the set.

Possible variation:

Canvas 1:  
Oversized headline.

Canvas 2:  
Compact headline with dominant mockup.

Canvas 3:  
One highlighted word.

Canvas 4:  
Text integrated with illustration.

Canvas 5:  
Minimal closing statement.

Variation can preserve attention while the typographic language remains coherent.

\---

\#\# Typography and Rhythm

Rhythm can be created through:

\- repeated scale relationships  
\- alternating dense and open text  
\- recurring line-break patterns  
\- repetition of specific words  
\- changes in alignment  
\- changes in emphasis

Evaluate the set together, not only each canvas independently.

\---

\#\# Expressive Typography

Typography may deliberately become expressive.

Possible techniques include:

\- oversized words  
\- dramatic contrast  
\- irregular line breaks  
\- rotation  
\- mixed weights  
\- selected italics  
\- cropped letters  
\- unexpected alignment  
\- word stacking  
\- repeated text  
\- text acting as background  
\- extreme scale contrast

These techniques should support the Art Direction.

Do not use expressive typography merely to make the screenshot look designed.

\---

\#\# Restraint

Creative typography can also be extremely restrained.

Examples:

one sentence  
\+  
large negative space

one small word  
\+  
oversized product

thin typography  
\+  
strong photography

Do not confuse typographic creativity with typographic complexity.

A single strong decision may be more effective than several effects.

\---

\#\# Breaking Typographic Rules

Conventional typographic rules exist for good reasons.

But they may be broken intentionally.

Possible intentional breaks:

\- extremely tight leading  
\- uneven line lengths  
\- partial cropping  
\- irregular alignment  
\- oversized text  
\- unconventional hierarchy  
\- mixed typographic voices

A rule break should create a visible benefit:

\- tension  
\- emphasis  
\- energy  
\- identity  
\- humor  
\- surprise

Do not break rules randomly.

Creative Lab should understand the expected effect before choosing the exception.

\---

\#\# Readability

Creativity must not accidentally destroy essential communication.

Consider:

\- contrast  
\- size  
\- spacing  
\- line length  
\- background complexity  
\- overlap  
\- crop

But readability is contextual.

Not every piece of text needs identical clarity.

Primary copy should usually be immediately readable.

Background or decorative typography may intentionally be partially obscured.

Distinguish:

information

from

visual texture.

\---

\#\# Shipper Constraints

Typography should remain executable with the typography capabilities available in Shipper.

Before relying on:

\- advanced distortion  
\- complex masking  
\- unsupported text effects  
\- variable font behavior  
\- custom blending  
\- uncommon rendering techniques

verify that Shipper can execute the intended treatment.

If a specific effect is unavailable, preserve the creative intention through supported techniques.

For example:

If text warping is unavailable, energy may still be created through:

\- scale  
\- rotation  
\- line breaking  
\- overlap  
\- color  
\- crop  
\- positioning

Do not reduce the Art Direction to a generic layout because one typographic technique is unavailable.

\---

\#\# What to Observe

Before execution, inspect:

\- message importance  
\- dominant words  
\- intended reading order  
\- typeface character  
\- scale relationships  
\- line breaks  
\- text block silhouette  
\- leading  
\- tracking  
\- alignment  
\- contrast  
\- relationship with mockups  
\- relationship with images  
\- available negative space  
\- potential overlap  
\- set-wide typographic rhythm

After execution, judge typography as part of the full rendered composition.

\---

\#\# Decision Logic

Do not ask:

"What font size should a headline be?"

Ask:

"What scale gives this headline the intended visual role?"

Do not ask:

"Should the headline be bold?"

Ask:

"What weight produces the intended visual mass and personality?"

Do not ask:

"Where should I break this line?"

Ask:

"What line structure creates the strongest combination of meaning and visual shape?"

Do not ask:

"Should the text be centered?"

Ask:

"What alignment best supports the composition?"

Do not ask:

"Is this unconventional?"

Ask:

"Does this typographic choice strengthen the Art Direction?"

\---

\#\# Trade-offs

Large type creates impact but consumes space.

Tight leading creates density but can reduce readability.

Wide tracking creates openness but can weaken word cohesion.

Multiple typefaces create contrast but can fragment the visual language.

Expressive line breaks create character but may slow reading.

High typographic consistency creates coherence but can become repetitive.

Strong variation creates rhythm but can weaken unity.

No trade-off is universally correct.

Choose according to the role of the canvas and the Art Direction.

\---

\#\# Anti-Patterns

Avoid defaulting to:

\#\#\# Headline always at the top

Typography may belong anywhere in the composition.

\#\#\# One automatic size hierarchy

Repeated scale relationships can make every design feel generic when chosen without regard to meaning. A familiar hierarchy can still be the strongest choice for a particular message.

\#\#\# Automatic line wrapping

Line breaks should be evaluated visually.

\#\#\# Everything bold

When everything is emphasized, nothing is emphasized.

\#\#\# Every word highlighted

Selective emphasis is usually stronger.

\#\#\# Text inside a box by default

A container should have a visual purpose.

\#\#\# Center alignment everywhere

Alignment should respond to composition.

\#\#\# Typography separated from imagery

Text can interact with mockups, illustrations, photography, and shapes.

\#\#\# Generic "large bold sans serif"

This is a useful language but not the definition of strong App Store screenshot typography.

\#\#\# Decorative effects without purpose

Effects cannot compensate for weak typographic hierarchy.

\#\#\# Invented editorial metadata

Avoid inventing decorative project labels, sequence numbers, category captions, pseudo-editorial metadata, or portfolio-style annotations unless they carry genuine product, brand, narrative, or informational meaning. A project name paired with an arbitrary frame number can look like a presentation annotation rather than useful communication to the viewer. Meaningful small labels remain valid; ask what the label contributes beyond the appearance of being designed.

\---

\#\# Visual Critique

After rendering, ask:

1\. What text do I read first?  
2\. Is that intentional?  
3\. Which word or phrase carries the strongest visual emphasis?  
4\. Is the hierarchy decisive enough?  
5\. Does the text block have an intentional shape?  
6\. Are the line breaks helping the message?  
7\. Is the typeface character consistent with the Art Direction?  
8\. Is the typography specific to the intention, whether restrained or expressive?  
9\. Is it expressive without reason?  
10\. Are too many elements competing for emphasis?  
11\. Does the typography interact with the composition or merely sit on top of it?  
12\. Does the mockup overpower the text?  
13\. Does the text overpower the product unnecessarily?  
14\. Could a stronger scale contrast improve the design?  
15\. Could one word become the visual idea?  
16\. Is negative space helping the typography?  
17\. Is any container unnecessary?  
18\. Does the text remain readable where it needs to?  
19\. Does this canvas feel typographically distinct from the previous one?  
20\. Does the full set have a coherent typographic language?

When small labels or numbers are present, consider whether they communicate something real or merely imitate editorial metadata. Would their removal clarify the message without losing meaning?

If the typography feels generic, do not immediately change the font.

First reconsider:

\- scale  
\- hierarchy  
\- line breaks  
\- weight  
\- alignment  
\- placement  
\- interaction  
\- contrast  
\- negative space

The problem may be typographic composition rather than typeface selection.

\---

\#\# Principle to Remember

Typography is not text styling.

Typography is the deliberate use of language as visual form.

Creative Lab should understand typography well enough to make it quiet, dominant, expressive, restrained, structured, fragmented, conventional, or unconventional according to the Art Direction.

\# Design Intelligence — Composition & Layout

\#\# Purpose

Composition determines how visual elements occupy space and relate to one another.

Layout is not simply arranging assets so everything fits.

It determines:

\- energy  
\- balance  
\- movement  
\- scale  
\- focus  
\- rhythm  
\- tension  
\- visual character

A composition can be clean, chaotic, symmetrical, asymmetric, dense, empty, static, dynamic, fragmented, continuous, editorial, playful, dramatic, or restrained.

There is no universal ideal layout.

The objective is to make spatial decisions that support the intended Art Direction.

\---

\#\# Core Principle

Do not compose by filling available space.

Compose by creating relationships.

Every major element should have a deliberate relationship with:

\- the canvas  
\- nearby elements  
\- distant elements  
\- negative space  
\- edges  
\- foreground and background  
\- the other canvases in the screenshot set

A composition should feel intentional even when it is unconventional.

\---

\#\# The Canvas Is an Active Boundary

The canvas is not merely a container.

Its edges can be used creatively.

An element may:

\- sit comfortably inside the canvas  
\- touch an edge  
\- leave the canvas  
\- enter from outside the canvas  
\- be cropped aggressively  
\- visually continue into another canvas  
\- appear too large for the canvas intentionally

Generous margins and edge tension are equally available. Neither containment nor leaving the canvas is evidence of creative quality by itself.

Edges can create tension and scale.

\---

\#\# Composition Modes

Creative Lab should understand many possible compositional structures.

Examples include:

\#\#\# Centered

Creates stability, clarity, formality, or iconic presence.

\#\#\# Asymmetric

Creates movement, tension, editorial character, or energy.

\#\#\# Split

Divides the canvas into distinct visual areas.

\#\#\# Full-bleed

Allows an image, color, screenshot, or mockup to dominate the entire surface.

\#\#\# Layered

Uses foreground, middle ground, and background elements.

\#\#\# Diagonal

Creates directional energy and movement.

\#\#\# Floating

Uses isolated objects with generous negative space.

\#\#\# Dense

Uses many closely interacting elements.

\#\#\# Oversized

Allows one element to exceed conventional scale.

\#\#\# Fragmented

Uses partial elements, crops, disconnected blocks, or controlled visual interruption.

\#\#\# Continuous

Allows visual elements to connect multiple canvases.

\#\#\# Typography-led

Uses text as a major spatial structure.

\#\#\# Product-led

Uses UI or mockups as the primary composition.

These are possibilities, not templates.

They may be combined.

\---

\#\# Grid

A grid can provide:

\- alignment  
\- rhythm  
\- proportion  
\- consistency  
\- structural clarity

But a grid is a tool, not a prison.

Elements can intentionally break the grid to:

\- create emphasis  
\- introduce energy  
\- differentiate a focal point  
\- create visual surprise  
\- produce a more editorial composition

Understand the underlying structure before breaking it.

Random misalignment is not the same as intentional grid-breaking.

\---

\#\# Alignment

Alignment creates relationships between elements.

Potential alignments include:

\- left  
\- right  
\- centered  
\- baseline  
\- vertical axis  
\- edge alignment  
\- internal alignment  
\- optical alignment

Not every element needs to share the same alignment.

Multiple alignments can coexist if the relationships remain intentional.

Optical alignment may differ from mathematical alignment.

An element can be mathematically centered yet visually feel off-center because of its shape or weight.

Judge the rendered composition visually.

\---

\#\# Balance

Balance is the distribution of visual weight.

Balance does not mean symmetry.

A large object on one side may be balanced by:

\- several smaller objects  
\- high-contrast typography  
\- strong color  
\- negative space  
\- a human figure  
\- visual density  
\- another element placed farther from the center

Possible balance types include:

\- symmetrical  
\- asymmetric  
\- radial  
\- dynamic  
\- deliberately unstable

A slightly unstable composition can create energy.

Perfect balance can create calm.

Neither is inherently better.

\---

\#\# Center of Gravity

Every composition has a perceived center of gravity.

Ask:

\- Where does the visual mass accumulate?  
\- Does the image feel like it is falling toward one side?  
\- Is that sensation intentional?  
\- Does another element counterbalance it?

Do not evaluate elements independently.

Evaluate the total visual mass.

\---

\#\# Scale

Scale is one of the strongest compositional tools.

Use scale to create:

\- dominance  
\- drama  
\- depth  
\- contrast  
\- intimacy  
\- exaggeration  
\- humor  
\- energy

Determine the device's role before deciding whether it should fit comfortably within the canvas.

A mockup may:

\- occupy almost the entire canvas  
\- be extremely small  
\- be cropped  
\- appear close to the viewer  
\- recede into the background  
\- be repeated at different scales

Scale contrast can create character, depth, or emphasis.

Similar scales can instead create stability, continuity, or restraint.

Choose the relationship that best supports the composition rather than treating stronger scale contrast as inherently more expressive.

\---

\#\# Cropping

Cropping is an intentional compositional decision.

Possible crops include:

\- partial device  
\- partial screenshot  
\- partial illustration  
\- partial face  
\- cropped typography  
\- elements entering from edges  
\- objects continuing beyond the visible canvas

Cropping can create:

\- scale  
\- movement  
\- depth  
\- mystery  
\- continuity  
\- confidence

Ask whether showing less or restoring more of the object creates a stronger relationship. Crop can follow from an appropriate scale and framing decision; it need not be the starting creative gesture.

\---

\#\# Off-Canvas Elements

An element can deliberately extend beyond the canvas.

This can make the design feel larger than the physical frame.

Useful for:

\- devices  
\- typography  
\- illustrations  
\- photos  
\- shapes  
\- decorative objects

Off-canvas placement can also help create continuity between screenshots.

The viewer should perceive intentional continuation rather than accidental clipping.

Off-canvas placement is one compositional option. It is useful when it materially improves scale, framing, movement, hierarchy, depth, or continuity, not merely when it makes the image appear more dynamic. A contained composition may express the same intention more naturally.

This describes intentional visual crop and off-canvas composition, not the semantics of any Shipper overflow setting. Implementation behavior comes from live Shipper capabilities. Inspect the final rendered or exported image to see whether the intended relationships survived, including the space around the headline and any unintended collisions.

\---

\#\# Overlap

Overlap creates relationships.

Objects may overlap to communicate:

\- depth  
\- connection  
\- priority  
\- movement  
\- integration

Examples:

\- headline partially behind a mockup  
\- mockup covering a decorative shape  
\- illustration crossing into UI  
\- UI card floating outside the device  
\- object spanning multiple depth layers

Avoid keeping every asset separated simply for clarity.

Separation is one option.

Interaction is another.

\---

\#\# Layering and Depth

Depth can make compositions richer.

Possible layers:

Background  
→ atmospheric or structural elements  
→ large supporting shapes  
→ product / screenshot  
→ floating UI  
→ typography  
→ decorative foreground elements

This order is not mandatory.

Typography can move behind objects.

UI can leave the device.

Foreground decoration can partially obscure content.

Depth should feel intentional and preserve the information that matters.

\---

\#\# Perspective and Rotation

Rotation can create:

\- motion  
\- informality  
\- tension  
\- depth  
\- playfulness  
\- directional flow

Perspective can create:

\- cinematic depth  
\- dimensionality  
\- a sense of physical object  
\- foreground/background separation

Avoid rotating mockups merely because dynamic design is desired.

Rotation should participate in the composition.

Consider what direction the object points toward and where it directs attention.

\---

\#\# Directional Flow

Elements can guide the eye through direction.

Useful directional elements include:

\- diagonals  
\- arrows  
\- gaze  
\- object orientation  
\- perspective lines  
\- curves  
\- repeated forms  
\- flight paths  
\- trails  
\- gestures  
\- typography angles

Directional flow can connect multiple areas or multiple canvases.

It can also intentionally push the eye outside the canvas toward the next screenshot.

\---

\#\# Negative Space

Negative space is active composition.

It can:

\- separate groups  
\- isolate a focal point  
\- create calm  
\- increase perceived scale  
\- produce tension  
\- guide attention  
\- make dense areas feel denser by contrast

Do not treat empty space as unfinished design.

Before filling it, identify its function.

\---

\#\# Density

A composition may be sparse or dense.

Sparse compositions can feel:

\- premium  
\- calm  
\- confident  
\- editorial  
\- minimal

Dense compositions can feel:

\- energetic  
\- playful  
\- rich  
\- youthful  
\- maximalist  
\- immersive

Density is not a quality score.

A dense composition can be excellent when hierarchy remains controlled.

A sparse or dense composition can be weak when it lacks character. Neither openness nor density supplies that character automatically.

Do not equate simplicity with quality.

\---

\#\# Typography as Composition

Text is not just content placed inside a layout.

Typography can create the layout itself.

It may:

\- occupy most of the canvas  
\- form strong geometric blocks  
\- interact with images  
\- overlap devices  
\- wrap around objects  
\- become oversized  
\- use dramatic line breaks  
\- run close to edges  
\- create vertical or diagonal movement

A word may become an image-like asset.

A headline may intentionally be too large to fit conventionally.

Do not restrict typography to a safe header region.

\---

\#\# Mockups as Composition

A device mockup is not automatically:

centered  
\+  
fully visible  
\+  
vertical  
\+  
below the headline.

Possible treatments include:

\- oversized  
\- cropped  
\- diagonal  
\- partially hidden  
\- entering from an edge  
\- multiple devices  
\- device crossing canvases  
\- close-up UI crop  
\- UI without obvious full-device emphasis  
\- device used as a structural diagonal  
\- device treated as a background surface

Choose the treatment based on the role of the product UI in the Art Direction.

Optical proportion depends on the rendered device silhouette, UI density, headline shape, surrounding space, and the combined center of gravity. Available canvas area alone is not a reason to enlarge the Mockup. Natural-looking scale is scale that feels appropriate to this relationship, not a fixed device-to-canvas ratio.

When the relationship feels mechanically placed, ask: What role does the Mockup play? How much visual weight should it carry? Does its scale support that role? Does its position feel optically intentional beside the headline? Is the crop improving the composition or merely making it look dynamic?

Judge those relationships in the rendered pixels rather than trusting coordinates alone. A smaller device, a different gap, or full containment can be as decisive as enlargement or an aggressive crop; choose the change that addresses the observed cause.

\---

\#\# UI Escaping the Device

When supported by available Shipper assets and execution capabilities, interface elements may visually escape the device.

Examples:

\- cards  
\- labels  
\- buttons  
\- messages  
\- metrics  
\- charts  
\- notifications  
\- feature callouts

This can make the product feel more tangible and integrate UI with the overall composition.

Do not use floating UI merely as decoration.

The extracted or recreated visual should support the story or composition.

\---

\#\# Images and Illustrations

Images and illustrations may function as:

\- background  
\- focal point  
\- supporting context  
\- character  
\- atmosphere  
\- texture  
\- visual bridge  
\- foreground element

Avoid automatically placing them beside a mockup.

They may interact directly with:

\- typography  
\- UI  
\- edges  
\- shapes  
\- other canvases

\---

\#\# Cross-Canvas Composition

An App Store screenshot set can be designed as individual canvases or as a connected visual sequence.

Elements may appear to continue across canvases.

Possible connections include:

\- one illustration crossing boundaries  
\- one oversized mockup spanning two canvases  
\- continuous background  
\- shared trajectory  
\- typography continuing across frames  
\- repeated shapes  
\- a visual object entering one canvas and resolving in another

Cross-canvas continuity can make the set feel intentionally designed as one system.

But it should not become mandatory.

Independent canvases may be stronger for some Art Directions.

\---

\#\# Variation Across the Set

A coherent set does not require repeated layout.

Avoid assuming that consistency means:

same headline position  
\+  
same mockup size  
\+  
same background  
\+  
same decoration

A set can remain coherent through:

\- typography  
\- palette  
\- asset treatment  
\- recurring shapes  
\- visual tone  
\- spacing behavior  
\- illustration style  
\- recurring compositional logic

while layouts change dramatically.

Example:

Canvas 1:  
Oversized typography.

Canvas 2:  
Full-screen mockup.

Canvas 3:  
Diagonal device with floating UI.

Canvas 4:  
Illustration-led composition.

Canvas 5:  
Minimal closing frame.

This variation can produce stronger rhythm than repeating one template.

The sequence illustrates variation, not a preferred progression of techniques. Variation can also come from quieter changes in product emphasis or spacing; a conventional arrangement remains legitimate wherever it communicates most strongly.

\---

\#\# Visual Rhythm Across Canvases

Consider the set as a sequence.

Possible rhythms:

dense → sparse → dense → sparse

dark → light → dark

typography-led → UI-led → image-led

large scale → close detail → large scale

static → dynamic → static

Rhythm can prevent visual fatigue.

Do not optimize every screenshot independently without evaluating the set together.

\---

\#\# Breaking the Grid

Breaking conventional layout rules can create strong design when intentional.

Possible techniques:

\- oversized text  
\- aggressive crop  
\- asymmetry  
\- edge collisions  
\- overlapping text and objects  
\- off-center focal points  
\- tilted devices  
\- text partially hidden  
\- intentionally irregular spacing  
\- objects spanning multiple canvases

Before breaking a rule, understand what visual effect the break creates.

The objective is not rebellion.

The objective is expression.

\---

\#\# Restraint

Creative composition does not require maximal complexity.

A bold decision can also be:

one enormous word

one tiny product screenshot

one unexpected empty region

one cropped device

one strong color field

one isolated UI element

Avoid assuming creativity means adding more assets.

Creativity often comes from relationships, scale, framing, or omission.

\---

\#\# Shipper Constraints

Art Direction should remain ambitious but executable.

Before committing to a composition, understand the available Shipper capabilities.

If an imagined effect cannot be executed directly:

\- simplify it  
\- approximate it  
\- reconstruct it using supported assets  
\- change the composition  
\- preserve the creative intention through another visual mechanism

Do not automatically abandon the Art Direction because one specific effect is unavailable.

Separate:

creative intention

from

specific implementation technique.

For example:

If advanced perspective distortion is unavailable, depth may still be created through:

\- scale  
\- rotation  
\- overlap  
\- crop  
\- shadow  
\- foreground objects

Preserve the idea when possible.

\---

\#\# What to Observe

Before execution, inspect:

\- canvas proportions  
\- dominant visual mass  
\- intended focal point  
\- empty regions  
\- edge interactions  
\- alignment systems  
\- overlap opportunities  
\- depth  
\- scale contrast  
\- directional movement  
\- typography geometry  
\- mockup role  
\- crop opportunities  
\- cross-canvas relationships  
\- rhythm across the set

Do not begin by asking:

"Where do I place the headline and mockup?"

First ask:

"What composition best expresses this idea?"

\---

\#\# Decision Logic

Do not ask:

"Should everything be aligned?"

Ask:

"Which relationships benefit from alignment and which benefit from tension?"

Do not ask:

"Is the mockup fully visible?"

Ask:

"How much of the mockup needs to be visible for the composition and message to work?"

Do not ask:

"Is this object too close to the edge?"

Ask:

"Does the edge interaction feel intentional?"

Do not ask:

"Is the layout symmetrical?"

Ask:

"Does the distribution of visual weight support the intended feeling?"

Do not ask:

"Should every screenshot use the same layout?"

Ask:

"What must remain coherent, and what can vary to create rhythm?"

\---

\#\# Trade-offs

Large scale creates impact but may hide product detail.

Dense layouts create energy but increase hierarchy complexity.

Minimal layouts improve focus but can become generic.

Cross-canvas continuity creates spectacle but may reduce the independence of individual screenshots.

Extreme crops create confidence but can reduce recognizability.

Strong asymmetry creates movement but may feel unstable.

Repeated layouts create coherence but can become monotonous.

High variation creates interest but can fragment the set.

No trade-off is automatically wrong.

Choose based on the Art Direction and the role of each canvas.

\---

\#\# Anti-Patterns

Avoid defaulting to:

\#\#\# Headline on top, mockup below

This is one possible layout, not the default architecture of an App Store screenshot.

\#\#\# Device always centered

Centering can remove energy when used automatically.

\#\#\# Device always fully visible

Cropping can create stronger scale and composition.

\#\#\# Every canvas uses the same template

A coherent Art Direction can support substantial layout variation.

\#\#\# Filling every empty area

Negative space may be intentional.

\#\#\# Keeping every asset separate

Overlap can create depth and integration.

\#\#\# Decorative rotation

Rotation without compositional purpose feels arbitrary.

\#\#\# Small adjustments without diagnosis

Structural weaknesses can benefit from decisive scale, crop, overlap, or position changes. An optical mismatch may need only a precise adjustment; the size of the edit is not a measure of creativity.

\#\#\# Grid obedience

A grid should support the design, not flatten it.

\#\#\# Creativity equals decoration

Adding stickers, symbols, shapes, or gradients does not automatically make a composition creative.

\---

\#\# Visual Critique

After rendering, inspect the composition as pixels.

Ask:

1\. Does the composition have a clear intention?  
2\. Does it feel designed or merely arranged?  
3\. Is the visual mass balanced in the intended way?  
4\. Does containment or off-canvas placement better support the intended framing?  
5\. Is the crop contributing to scale and hierarchy, or unnecessarily hiding product evidence?  
6\. Does any edge interaction improve the composition rather than signal dynamism alone?  
7\. Does the device's scale and position feel natural in relation to the headline and visual center of gravity?  
8\. Could typography participate more strongly in the composition?  
9\. Are overlaps meaningful?  
10\. Is the negative space intentional?  
11\. Is there enough scale contrast?  
12\. Does the composition feel static when it should feel dynamic?  
13\. Does it feel busy when it should feel restrained?  
14\. Are decorative elements compensating for a weak composition?  
15\. Is the canvas too similar to the previous one?  
16\. Does the set have visual rhythm?  
17\. Could one canvas deliberately break the established pattern?  
18\. Is the Art Direction visible through composition alone?  
19\. Have Shipper limitations caused the idea to become generic?  
20\. Can another supported technique preserve the original creative intention?

If the result feels generic, do not immediately add decoration.

First reconsider:

\- scale  
\- crop  
\- hierarchy  
\- position  
\- overlap  
\- negative space  
\- typography  
\- rhythm

Strong composition usually matters more than additional assets.

\---

\#\# Principle to Remember

Composition is not the act of fitting assets inside a canvas.

It is the deliberate creation of relationships between space, scale, objects, typography, UI, and visual weight.

Creative Lab should understand conventional composition well enough to use it, stretch it, or break it intentionally when the Art Direction benefits from doing so.

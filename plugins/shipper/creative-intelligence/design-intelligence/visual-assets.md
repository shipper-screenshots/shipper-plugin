\# Design Intelligence — Visual Assets

\#\# Purpose

Visual assets are the materials Creative Lab uses to build a composition.

Within Shipper, this may include:

\- Mockups  
\- Screenshot Sources  
\- text  
\- SF Symbols  
\- icons  
\- illustrations  
\- images  
\- photography  
\- shapes  
\- backgrounds  
\- gradients  
\- decorative elements

The objective is not to use as many assets as possible.

The objective is to understand the role each asset plays in the Art Direction and composition.

\---

\#\# Core Principle

Every visible asset should have a role.

Possible roles include:

\- focal point  
\- product proof  
\- context  
\- hierarchy  
\- structure  
\- depth  
\- movement  
\- atmosphere  
\- storytelling  
\- decoration

Decoration is a valid role.

But decorative assets should still contribute intentionally to the visual language.

Do not add elements simply because a region looks empty.

\---

\#\# Role Before Asset

Do not begin with:

"What icon can I add?"

Begin with:

"What does this composition need?"

Then determine whether the need is best solved by:

\- typography  
\- mockup  
\- illustration  
\- shape  
\- color  
\- negative space  
\- image  
\- icon  
\- nothing

The absence of an asset can be a deliberate design decision.

\---

\#\# Mockups

A Mockup is not automatically the main visual element.

Possible roles:

\- hero  
\- proof  
\- supporting object  
\- framing device  
\- compositional shape  
\- background element  
\- repeated motif  
\- visual bridge

Its treatment should depend on its role.

\---

\#\# Mockup Scale

A mockup can be:

\- enormous  
\- small  
\- partially visible  
\- tightly cropped  
\- isolated  
\- repeated

A large mockup can communicate:

\- immediacy  
\- confidence  
\- product emphasis

A small mockup can communicate:

\- restraint  
\- context  
\- contrast  
\- spatial drama

Choose visibility and scale according to the product evidence needed and the Mockup's role. Neither the largest possible device nor an oversized crop is a default sign of creative strength.

Consider its optical proportion to the headline, surrounding space, and the composition's center of gravity. The frame and UI detail contribute weight as well as size. A Mockup may occupy less canvas and still communicate more clearly when its position and weight feel natural. Inspect the rendered relationship rather than relying on dimensions or coordinates alone.

\---

\#\# Mockup Cropping

The full device does not need to remain visible.

Possible crops:

\- top only  
\- bottom only  
\- side crop  
\- extreme zoom  
\- diagonal fragment

Cropping can increase perceived scale and integrate the device more strongly into the composition.

The Screenshot Source should remain understandable enough for its intended role.

Let crop follow from the role, scale, and framing of the composition. Off-canvas placement can improve scale, framing, movement, hierarchy, depth, or continuity; apparent dynamism alone does not justify it. Full containment remains an equally valid treatment. These are visual concepts, not definitions of Shipper's implementation-specific overflow behavior. The final rendered or exported composition is the evidence for whether the crop preserves the intended headline, device, and edge relationships.

\---

\#\# Mockup Rotation

Rotation can create:

\- motion  
\- energy  
\- perspective  
\- playfulness  
\- visual flow

Rotation should interact with the rest of the composition.

Do not tilt devices automatically to make them "dynamic."

Ask what direction the angle creates and where it guides attention.

\---

\#\# Multiple Mockups

Multiple devices can communicate:

\- comparison  
\- sequence  
\- social interaction  
\- breadth  
\- continuity  
\- scale

They can be:

\- aligned  
\- stacked  
\- overlapping  
\- different scales  
\- entering from different edges

Avoid creating multiple-device compositions without a clear reason.

More devices increase density and competition.

\---

\#\# Screenshot Sources

The Screenshot Source is the user's actual app screenshot contained in a Mockup.

It is product evidence.

Do not confuse it with:

\- App Store Reference  
\- decorative imagery  
\- unrelated visual inspiration

The screenshot content itself should influence composition.

Observe:

\- dominant colors  
\- visual density  
\- important UI area  
\- text inside UI  
\- focal feature  
\- existing whitespace  
\- high-contrast regions

Do not treat all screenshots as interchangeable rectangles.

\---

\#\# Product Detail

Sometimes the full device is less useful than a specific UI detail.

When Shipper capabilities allow an appropriate treatment, emphasize:

\- card  
\- metric  
\- conversation  
\- chart  
\- control  
\- result  
\- visual state

The goal is to make the product proof understandable.

Do not show the full device merely because it is available.

\---

\#\# Feature Snippets

A Feature Snippet can emphasize or clarify a detail, establish hierarchy, provide a useful close-up, create a new compositional relationship, or make product proof readable when it would otherwise be too small. Its value depends on what it adds alongside the Mockup.

Before extracting or emphasizing one, consider: What does this snippet add that the Mockup does not already communicate clearly? Repeating already-clear UI can consume space, split attention, and weaken the screenshot. Repetition may still be useful when the changed scale or relationship creates a meaningful emphasis; extraction itself is not the benefit.

In the rendered composition, ask whether the snippet adds information or merely repeats the Mockup, and whether removing it would make the composition stronger. Keep or omit it according to that contribution, not as a recurring treatment across the set.

\---

\#\# SF Symbols and Icons

Icons can:

\- reinforce meaning  
\- create rhythm  
\- act as directional cues  
\- support hierarchy  
\- create a motif  
\- become decorative graphic elements

Consider:

\- optical size  
\- weight  
\- style consistency  
\- repetition  
\- color  
\- surrounding space

Do not add icons beside every text label by default.

Icons and SF Symbols are available visual material even when the user has not specifically requested them. They may supply a metaphor, directional cue, hierarchy, rhythm, personality, storytelling, or a visual bridge. Choose them when that role helps the intention, not automatically to make a screenshot look creative and never merely because an area looks empty. A set with few or no icons can be fully resolved.

\---

\#\# Icons as Graphic Objects

An icon does not need to remain small.

It may become:

\- oversized  
\- cropped  
\- repeated  
\- background texture  
\- focal element

A simple symbol at extreme scale can create a strong visual concept.

But recognizability and relevance should remain intentional.

\---

\#\# Illustrations

Illustrations can provide:

\- personality  
\- narrative  
\- emotion  
\- metaphor  
\- context  
\- movement  
\- visual differentiation

Determine whether the illustration is:

\- hero  
\- supporting  
\- background  
\- bridge  
\- decorative motif

Do not combine unrelated illustration styles within one Art Direction without purpose.

\---

\#\# Illustration and Product

Illustration may:

\- surround the mockup  
\- interact with the mockup  
\- point toward UI  
\- sit behind the device  
\- overlap the device  
\- extend across canvases

Avoid the default composition:

illustration on one side  
\+  
device on the other

unless that relationship is intentional.

\---

\#\# Photography

Photography can communicate:

\- people  
\- context  
\- lifestyle  
\- scale  
\- emotion  
\- environment

Observe:

\- focal point  
\- subject position  
\- gaze  
\- color  
\- contrast  
\- crop  
\- negative space

Photography already contains a composition.

Creative Lab should work with it rather than treating it as a neutral rectangle.

\---

\#\# Human Subjects

Faces and people naturally attract attention.

A person may become the dominant focal point even if the product was intended to dominate.

This can be useful.

For example, emotional outcome may be stronger than interface detail.

But dominance should be intentional.

\---

\#\# Shapes

Shapes can create:

\- structure  
\- grouping  
\- frames  
\- movement  
\- depth  
\- emphasis  
\- visual identity

Possible shapes include:

\- circles  
\- rectangles  
\- organic forms  
\- lines  
\- large color fields  
\- cropped geometry

Shapes should not exist solely as filler.

Ask what compositional role they perform.

\---

\#\# Shapes as Containers

A shape may contain:

\- text  
\- UI  
\- icon  
\- metric  
\- image

But not every element needs a card, bubble, or pill.

Use containers when they strengthen:

\- hierarchy  
\- grouping  
\- contrast  
\- Art Direction

Avoid excessive UI-like containers in marketing compositions.

\---

\#\# Decorative Elements

Decoration can contribute:

\- personality  
\- atmosphere  
\- richness  
\- humor  
\- movement  
\- cultural tone

Examples:

\- stars  
\- sparkles  
\- arrows  
\- stickers  
\- lines  
\- dots  
\- abstract shapes

Decoration is not forbidden.

But it should not compensate for weak composition.

Strong composition first.

Decoration second.

\---

\#\# Asset Scale Contrast

Mixing scales can create visual energy.

For example:

oversized illustration

\+  
tiny icon

\+  
medium device

or

huge SF Symbol

\+  
small headline

Strong scale contrast can make simple assets feel highly creative.

\---

\#\# Asset Interaction

Assets should not always remain isolated.

They can:

\- overlap  
\- obscure  
\- frame  
\- interrupt  
\- connect  
\- cross boundaries  
\- visually respond to one another

Interaction makes the composition feel designed as a whole.

\---

\#\# Foreground and Background

Assets can occupy different depth roles.

Background:

\- color fields  
\- patterns  
\- large low-contrast shapes  
\- atmospheric imagery

Middle ground:

\- mockups  
\- illustrations  
\- typography

Foreground:

\- floating UI  
\- icons  
\- shapes  
\- cropped decorative elements

Depth ordering may change according to the Art Direction.

\---

\#\# Repetition

Repeating an asset can create:

\- pattern  
\- rhythm  
\- abundance  
\- movement  
\- visual identity

Repeated devices, icons, or shapes may become a compositional system.

But repetition should have a reason.

Do not duplicate assets merely to fill space.

\---

\#\# Consistency of Asset Language

A coherent set may share:

\- illustration style  
\- icon treatment  
\- shadow behavior  
\- geometry  
\- mockup treatment  
\- decorative motif

Consistency does not require every canvas to use every asset type.

One screenshot may contain no illustration.

Another may be illustration-led.

They can still belong to the same Art Direction.

\---

\#\# Contrast of Asset Types

Different asset types can create useful contrast.

Examples:

clean UI  
\+  
organic illustration

precise typography  
\+  
playful stickers

photography  
\+  
flat graphic shapes

simple icon  
\+  
large realistic device

Contrast can create identity when the relationship is intentional.

\---

\#\# Removing Assets

Creative Lab should actively consider removal.

Ask:

\- Does this element contribute?  
\- Does it improve hierarchy?  
\- Does it strengthen Art Direction?  
\- Does it communicate anything?  
\- Does it create needed rhythm?

If not, removing it may improve the composition.

\---

\#\# Asset Availability

Creative ambition should remain aware of what Shipper can actually provide.

Before committing to an asset-dependent idea, determine whether the required material exists through:

\- user-provided assets  
\- Screenshot Sources  
\- SF Symbols  
\- Shipper Asset Library  
\- available illustrations  
\- available images  
\- available shapes  
\- supported text

Do not invent unavailable assets as if they already exist.

\---

\#\# Shipper Constraints

If an ideal asset treatment cannot be executed, preserve the visual intention through supported alternatives.

Examples:

Advanced 3D element unavailable:

use scale \+ overlap \+ shadow \+ rotation.

Complex custom illustration unavailable:

use simple shapes \+ iconography \+ typography.

Advanced mask unavailable:

use crop \+ foreground overlap.

Complex compositing unavailable:

use layering \+ contrast.

Creative Lab should distinguish:

the idea

from

the specific production technique.

\---

\#\# What to Observe

Before execution, inspect:

\- available assets  
\- asset roles  
\- visual style  
\- screenshot content  
\- mockup importance  
\- illustration relevance  
\- icon consistency  
\- possible scale contrast  
\- overlap opportunities  
\- depth  
\- unnecessary decoration  
\- cross-canvas asset motifs

\---

\#\# Decision Logic

Do not ask:

"What assets can I add?"

Ask:

"What visual role needs to be fulfilled?"

Do not ask:

"Should the device be large?"

Ask:

"What role should the product play in this canvas?"

Do not ask:

"Should I add decoration?"

Ask:

"Does the composition need additional visual energy or character?"

Do not ask:

"Should I show the entire screenshot?"

Ask:

"What product evidence is necessary?"

\---

\#\# Trade-offs

More assets create richness but increase complexity.

Fewer assets create clarity but may reduce narrative possibilities.

Large mockups show product strongly but consume composition space.

Small mockups allow expressive design but reduce UI detail.

Illustrations create personality but may compete with product evidence.

Photography creates emotional context but may dominate hierarchy.

Decoration creates character but can create noise.

No trade-off is universally correct.

\---

\#\# Anti-Patterns

Avoid defaulting to:

\#\#\# Device as mandatory hero

The product can be shown in many roles.

\#\#\# Full device every time

Crop and detail can be stronger.

\#\#\# Icon beside every statement

Icons should have a visual purpose.

\#\#\# Decoration to fix emptiness

Negative space may be stronger.

\#\#\# Too many unrelated asset styles

Visual language should feel intentional.

\#\#\# Every asset fully visible

Partial visibility can create scale and movement.

\#\#\# Everything isolated

Interaction and overlap can create richer composition.

\#\#\# More assets means more creative

Creativity can come from scale, typography, spacing, or omission.

\---

\#\# Visual Critique

After rendering, ask:

1\. What role does each major asset play?  
2\. Is any asset unnecessary?  
3\. Does the Mockup's role justify its optical scale and position relative to the headline?  
4\. Is the product visible enough for its intended role?  
5\. Does the crop improve the composition, or would showing more create a stronger relationship?  
6\. Are assets interacting or merely sitting beside each other?  
7\. Is illustration supporting or competing?  
8\. Are icons optically consistent?  
9\. Is decoration compensating for weak composition?  
10\. Is scale contrast strong enough?  
11\. Does depth feel intentional?  
12\. Are asset styles coherent?  
13\. Could removing one element make the design stronger?  
14\. Are Shipper limitations visible in the final design?  
15\. Could another supported asset treatment preserve the original Art Direction better?

For a Feature Snippet, what is gained beyond the UI already visible in the Mockup? For an icon, what visual or narrative role is gained? Consider removal when either repeats or decorates without a useful contribution.

\---

\#\# Principle to Remember

Assets are materials, not solutions.

Creative Lab should choose, scale, crop, combine, overlap, repeat, simplify, or remove assets according to the Art Direction rather than according to a default screenshot formula.

\# Design Intelligence — Visual Critique

\#\# Purpose

Visual Critique allows Creative Lab to evaluate what was actually rendered.

A design may be logically correct in planning and still fail visually.

The preview is therefore evidence.

Creative Lab must inspect the pixels, identify problems, understand their causes, and decide whether refinement is necessary.

Critique is not the search for perfection.

It is the ability to distinguish:

\- intentional choices  
\- accidental outcomes  
\- weak relationships  
\- successful relationships  
\- unnecessary complexity  
\- missed creative opportunities

\---

\#\# Core Principle

Judge the rendered image, not the intention.

Do not defend a design because the planned reasoning sounded correct.

If the pixels contradict the intended hierarchy, Art Direction, balance, or readability, the pixels are the reality.

Visual Critique should answer:

1\. What is happening?  
2\. Why is it happening?  
3\. Is it intentional?  
4\. Does it support the Art Direction?  
5\. What is the smallest or strongest change that would improve it?

\---

\#\# Observe Before Editing

Do not modify the canvas immediately after noticing discomfort.

First diagnose the issue.

Bad pattern:

"The headline feels weak.  
Make it bigger."

Better reasoning:

"The mockup attracts attention first because it is significantly larger, higher contrast, and centrally positioned.

The headline was intended to establish the frame.

Possible corrections:

\- reduce mockup dominance  
\- increase headline isolation  
\- change scale contrast  
\- reposition the mockup  
\- alter background contrast"

Identify cause before treatment.

\---

\#\# First Impression

Evaluate the screenshot quickly.

Ask:

\- What do I see first?  
\- What feeling does it create?  
\- Does it feel intentional?  
\- Is there immediate visual character?  
\- Does it look generic?  
\- Does anything feel obviously awkward?

The first impression matters because many visual hierarchy problems appear before detailed inspection.

\---

\#\# Hierarchy Critique

Inspect:

\- focal point  
\- secondary anchors  
\- attention path  
\- competing elements  
\- dominant color regions  
\- dominant typography  
\- mockup dominance

Ask:

\- Is attention going where intended?  
\- Is the hierarchy decisive?  
\- Are two elements unintentionally fighting?  
\- Is any secondary element too strong?

If hierarchy fails, adjust the relationship rather than automatically enlarging the headline.

\---

\#\# Composition Critique

Inspect:

\- visual center of gravity  
\- balance  
\- alignment  
\- scale  
\- crop  
\- overlap  
\- edge relationships  
\- negative space  
\- depth

Ask:

\- Does this feel composed or merely arranged?  
\- Does containment or off-canvas placement better serve the intended framing?  
\- Does the layout communicate the intention, whether familiar or unexpected?  
\- Would more or less crop or scale improve the relationship?  
\- Does asymmetry feel intentional?  
\- Are elements interacting?

When a Mockup feels mechanically placed, consider its role and intended weight before its size. Does its rendered scale support that role? Does the position feel optically intentional beside the headline and within the full center of gravity? Is the crop improving the composition or merely making it appear dynamic?

Inspect whether the final rendered or exported composition preserves that relationship without unintended collisions, awkward near-tangencies, or lost product evidence. Visual crop and off-canvas intent do not define the behavior of Shipper's overflow setting; coordinates or a setting alone cannot establish visual success.

\---

\#\# Typography Critique

Inspect:

\- hierarchy  
\- line breaks  
\- scale  
\- weight  
\- leading  
\- tracking  
\- alignment  
\- block shape  
\- emphasis  
\- relationship to assets

Ask:

\- Does typography participate in the composition?  
\- Is the most important wording visually important?  
\- Are line breaks intentional?  
\- Does the typography feel too generic?  
\- Is emphasis overused?  
\- Is essential text readable?

Do small labels, project names, sequence numbers, category captions, or portfolio-style annotations convey genuine product, brand, narrative, or informational meaning? If they merely simulate editorial metadata, their removal may strengthen the composition. Small meaningful labels are not a problem in themselves.

\---

\#\# Color Critique

Inspect:

\- dominance  
\- contrast  
\- value  
\- saturation  
\- temperature  
\- accent  
\- background/product relationship

Ask:

\- What color dominates?  
\- Is contrast located where attention should go?  
\- Does the color behavior support the intended atmosphere?  
\- Are colors competing?  
\- Is an accent overused?  
\- Does the app UI belong to the surrounding palette?

Are UI colors being amplified because they serve the Art Direction or simply because they are present? Would a neutral or softer tonal environment let the product speak more clearly? Restraint and amplification are alternatives to evaluate, not category formulas.

\---

\#\# Spacing Critique

Inspect:

\- grouping  
\- separation  
\- negative space  
\- margins  
\- density  
\- near-collisions  
\- edge tension

Ask:

\- Which elements feel related?  
\- Is that intended?  
\- Is empty space useful?  
\- Is anything awkwardly almost touching?  
\- Are regions too evenly distributed?  
\- Could stronger density contrast improve the design?

\---

\#\# Visual Weight Critique

Inspect:

\- heavy regions  
\- light regions  
\- center of gravity  
\- counterweights  
\- saturation  
\- density  
\- scale

Ask:

\- What feels heaviest?  
\- Does it deserve that weight?  
\- Does the degree of balance suit the intention?  
\- Is imbalance creating energy or looking accidental?

\---

\#\# Asset Critique

Inspect:

\- Mockups  
\- Screenshot Sources  
\- illustrations  
\- icons  
\- images  
\- shapes  
\- decorative elements

Ask:

\- Does each major asset have a role?  
\- Is anything unnecessary?  
\- Does the Mockup treatment serve its role, whether conventional or unusual?  
\- Is product evidence clear enough?  
\- Are decorative assets compensating for weak design?  
\- Are asset styles coherent?

If there is a Feature Snippet, is it adding information, emphasis, or a useful close-up, or merely repeating UI already clear in the Mockup? Would removing it make the composition stronger? If there is an icon, what role does it perform beyond filling space?

\---

\#\# Art Direction Critique

Ask:

\- Is the Art Direction visible?  
\- Could this design belong to almost any app?  
\- Does it have character?  
\- Are the choices coherent?  
\- Is it relying on a generic aesthetic?  
\- Is there a clear visual attitude?  
\- Does the design feel specific rather than generated from familiar defaults?

If Art Direction is weak, local polishing may not solve the problem.

The composition may need a more substantial rethink.

\---

\#\# Creative Quality

Evaluate whether the design does more than avoid mistakes.

Ask:

\- Is there a memorable decision?  
\- Is there visual surprise?  
\- Is anything distinctive?  
\- Is there meaningful scale contrast?  
\- Is the product presented in an interesting way?  
\- Does the design exploit the canvas?  
\- Is the design specific to the intended experience?

A clean design can still be creatively weak.

Do not confuse absence of errors with high creative quality.

Nor is surprise a requirement for every canvas. A familiar, restrained composition may be the strongest expression of the intention. These questions support diagnosis; they are not a checklist of effects to add or criteria requiring every image to become more expressive.

\---

\#\# Intentionality

For unusual choices, ask:

\- Does this feel deliberate?  
\- Does the choice reinforce the Art Direction?  
\- Would a viewer perceive this as confidence or mistake?

Examples:

\- aggressive crop  
\- irregular spacing  
\- overlapping text  
\- tilted device  
\- large empty region  
\- unusual color

An unconventional choice needs a visible benefit to the intended experience, not only the appearance of intentionality. The same test applies to a conventional choice.

\---

\#\# Restraint

Critique should also detect overdesign.

Ask:

\- Can anything be removed?  
\- Is there unnecessary decoration?  
\- Are too many techniques competing?  
\- Is every area trying to attract attention?  
\- Would one strong visual decision be enough?

Refinement often means subtraction.

\---

\#\# Genericity

Watch for patterns that may indicate generic design:

\- centered device  
\- headline above  
\- gradient background  
\- small decorative shapes  
\- soft glow  
\- identical composition across canvases  
\- uniform typography  
\- safe scale  
\- excessive symmetry

These patterns are not automatically wrong.

They are a warning only when no specific visual reason exists.

Also watch for convergence toward huge bold typography, asymmetric editorial layouts, flat color fields, strong crops, UI extraction, and poster-like composition. These can become familiar defaults just as easily as a centered device on a gradient. Do not confuse frequently described Design Intelligence techniques with Shipper's preferred style.

Ask whether the app and creative intention explain the choices, or whether the knowledge's familiar examples are driving them. Restrained, illustrative, photographic, playful, minimal, dense, typographic, product-led, elegant, expressive, conventional, and unconventional directions remain equally legitimate. Detecting repetition across work is useful when evidence is available; it does not require forced novelty or abandoning a direction that fits.

\---

\#\# Screenshot Set Critique

Do not evaluate canvases only in isolation.

Review the complete ordered set.

Inspect:

\- coherence  
\- variation  
\- rhythm  
\- repetition  
\- density  
\- color progression  
\- mockup treatment  
\- typography variation  
\- visual fatigue

Ask:

\- Do canvases feel related?  
\- Are they too similar?  
\- Is variation too random?  
\- Does the set have rhythm?  
\- Is one canvas significantly weaker?  
\- Does each image contribute something visually distinct?  
\- Is there a visual climax or reset where useful?

\---

\#\# Compare Intention and Result

For each important decision, compare:

Intention:  
"The typography should dominate."

Result:  
"The device dominates."

Intention:  
"The composition should feel energetic."

Result:  
"The layout feels centered and static."

Intention:  
"The palette should feel restrained."

Result:  
"Multiple saturated accents compete."

This gap between intention and result identifies where refinement is needed.

\---

\#\# Diagnose the Cause

When a problem is found, identify the most likely cause.

Possible causes:

\- scale  
\- contrast  
\- position  
\- spacing  
\- alignment  
\- color  
\- density  
\- asset choice  
\- crop  
\- typography  
\- depth  
\- repetition

Do not modify multiple variables blindly.

Understand the relationship first.

\---

\#\# Refinement Strength

Not every problem requires a small adjustment.

Some issues need:

\#\#\# Micro correction

Examples:

\- optical spacing  
\- slight alignment  
\- line break  
\- small scale adjustment

\#\#\# Structural correction

Examples:

\- mockup too dominant  
\- weak hierarchy  
\- poor grouping  
\- balance problem

\#\#\# Art Direction correction

Examples:

\- generic overall result  
\- composition contradicts intended character  
\- set lacks visual identity

Choose the level of correction according to the actual problem.

\---

\#\# Avoid Endless Refinement

Not every imperfection needs correction.

A design may contain:

\- tension  
\- irregularity  
\- asymmetry  
\- roughness

that supports the Art Direction.

Do not polish intentional character away.

Stop when:

\- hierarchy works  
\- Art Direction is visible  
\- composition feels intentional  
\- essential information is legible  
\- the set is coherent  
\- remaining imperfections are acceptable or expressive

\---

\#\# Shipper Capability Awareness

If the ideal correction requires unsupported functionality, ask:

"What supported change preserves the same visual intention?"

Possible alternatives:

\- scale instead of distortion  
\- crop instead of masking  
\- overlap instead of advanced compositing  
\- color contrast instead of lighting  
\- rotation instead of 3D perspective  
\- typography instead of custom illustration

Do not accept generic output solely because Shipper lacks one advanced effect.

\---

\#\# What to Observe

During every critique pass, inspect:

\- first impression  
\- hierarchy  
\- composition  
\- typography  
\- color  
\- spacing  
\- visual weight  
\- assets  
\- Art Direction  
\- creative quality  
\- set-wide rhythm  
\- execution constraints

Do not assume every category needs correction.

Focus on the relationships that materially affect the result.

\---

\#\# Decision Logic

Do not ask:

"Is this good?"

Ask:

"What is working, what is not, and why?"

Do not ask:

"Should I improve everything?"

Ask:

"Which change would most improve the intended result?"

Do not ask:

"Does this follow design rules?"

Ask:

"Does this visual decision support the Art Direction?"

Do not ask:

"Is this unconventional?"

Ask:

"Does it feel intentional?"

Do not ask:

"Can I polish this?"

Ask:

"Is polishing actually the right level of correction?"

\---

\#\# Trade-offs

More refinement can improve precision but remove spontaneity.

Stronger hierarchy can improve clarity but reduce visual ambiguity.

Simplification can improve focus but remove character.

Additional complexity can create richness but increase noise.

Consistency can create coherence but reduce variation.

No trade-off is automatically correct.

\---

\#\# Anti-Patterns

Avoid:

\#\#\# Editing before diagnosis

Identify cause first.

\#\#\# Automatically enlarging text

Hierarchy problems may come from other elements.

\#\#\# Adding decoration to fix genericity

Weak Art Direction cannot usually be solved with extra stickers.

\#\#\# Correcting every asymmetry

Some imbalance is intentional.

\#\#\# Making every canvas consistent

Coherence does not require duplication.

\#\#\# Endless micro-adjustments

Structural problems need structural changes.

\#\#\# Defending intention over pixels

The rendered result is what matters.

\#\#\# AI self-congratulation

Do not assume the design is strong because the reasoning was sophisticated.

Evaluate the actual visual result.

\---

\#\# Visual Critique Questions

After rendering, ask:

1\. What do I see first?  
2\. Is that intentional?  
3\. What feeling does the canvas create?  
4\. Is the Art Direction visible?  
5\. Does it feel specific to this product?  
6\. Does it feel composed rather than arranged?  
7\. Is the visual hierarchy strong?  
8\. Is the visual weight intentional?  
9\. Does typography participate in the design?  
10\. Does color have clear roles?  
11\. Is negative space purposeful?  
12\. Are assets interacting meaningfully?  
13\. Does the Mockup's optical scale and position support its role and relationship to the headline?  
14\. Is product evidence clear enough?  
15\. Is anything unnecessary?  
16\. Is anything chosen from habit rather than for the product and intention?  
17\. Is anything expressive without reason?  
18\. Does unusual behavior feel intentional?  
19\. Could removing something improve the result?  
20\. Could one stronger creative decision improve the result?  
21\. Does this canvas differ enough from the others?  
22\. Does the full set have rhythm?  
23\. Is any canvas significantly weaker?  
24\. Are Shipper limitations visibly reducing the Art Direction?  
25\. Is there a supported alternative that would preserve the idea better?

\---

\#\# Principle to Remember

Visual Critique is not the search for rule compliance.

It is the ability to look at the actual pixels, understand why they behave as they do, and make deliberate decisions about what should remain, change, simplify, or become more ambitious.

Creative Lab should trust the rendered result over the planned intention.

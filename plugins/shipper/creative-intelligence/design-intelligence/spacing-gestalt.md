\# Design Intelligence — Spacing & Gestalt

\#\# Purpose

Spacing determines how elements relate, separate, group, breathe, and compete.

Gestalt principles explain how the eye naturally interprets visual relationships.

Together, they help Creative Lab control:

\- grouping  
\- separation  
\- clarity  
\- density  
\- rhythm  
\- hierarchy  
\- visual tension  
\- perceived structure

Spacing is not leftover space between assets.

Empty space is part of the design.

\---

\#\# Core Principle

Do not ask:

"How much spacing should I use?"

Ask:

"What relationship should the viewer perceive between these elements?"

Elements placed close together tend to feel related.

Elements separated by significant space tend to feel independent.

Spacing therefore communicates meaning before the viewer consciously reads the content.

There is no universally correct spacing scale.

The correct relationship depends on the Art Direction and composition.

\---

\#\# Proximity

Proximity is one of the strongest grouping mechanisms.

Elements close together are usually perceived as belonging together.

This can connect:

\- headline and supporting copy  
\- label and metric  
\- icon and text  
\- UI card and explanation  
\- illustration and related message  
\- several visual fragments into one unit

Elements do not require boxes or borders to become a group.

Proximity alone may be enough.

Avoid adding containers when spacing can communicate the relationship more elegantly.

\---

\#\# Separation

Distance can communicate distinction.

Increase separation when elements should feel:

\- independent  
\- sequential  
\- contrasting  
\- conceptually different  
\- visually secondary

Large separation can also create drama.

For example:

small headline

\+  
very large empty region

\+  
oversized mockup entering from below

may create stronger tension than distributing the elements evenly.

Do not assume unused space should be reduced.

\---

\#\# Negative Space

Negative space is active visual material.

It can:

\- isolate a focal point  
\- separate groups  
\- create calm  
\- increase perceived scale  
\- create tension  
\- improve hierarchy  
\- make dense regions more powerful  
\- direct attention

Negative space may exist inside, around, or between elements.

Do not evaluate empty space as absence.

Ask what it contributes.

\---

\#\# Internal and External Spacing

Distinguish between:

\#\#\# Internal spacing

Space inside a visual group.

Examples:

\- between headline lines  
\- inside a text block  
\- inside a card  
\- between icon and label

\#\#\# External spacing

Space between groups or major composition zones.

External spacing should usually create a stronger separation than internal spacing when the groups are conceptually distinct.

But this is not a fixed numerical rule.

The visual difference is what matters.

\---

\#\# Density

Spacing controls density.

Dense compositions can feel:

\- energetic  
\- immersive  
\- expressive  
\- playful  
\- editorial  
\- urgent  
\- maximalist

Open compositions can feel:

\- calm  
\- refined  
\- premium  
\- confident  
\- spacious  
\- focused

Neither is inherently superior.

A highly creative screenshot may be intentionally dense.

The important question is whether the density is controlled.

\---

\#\# Density Contrast

A strong composition can combine dense and sparse regions.

For example:

large empty background

\+  
one dense cluster of UI cards

creates a strong focal area.

Or:

dense typography

\+  
isolated product mockup

can create contrast.

Uniform density often reduces visual tension.

Consider whether the composition benefits from contrasting spatial behaviors.

\---

\#\# Gestalt — Proximity

Objects close together are perceived as belonging together.

Use proximity intentionally to communicate structure.

Do not rely only on:

\- borders  
\- containers  
\- background boxes  
\- separators

to communicate grouping.

Spacing may create cleaner and stronger relationships.

\---

\#\# Gestalt — Similarity

Elements that share visual characteristics are perceived as related.

Similarity can come from:

\- color  
\- shape  
\- scale  
\- typography  
\- icon style  
\- orientation  
\- treatment

Repeated similarity can create a system even when elements are spatially separated.

This is useful across a screenshot set.

For example, repeated colored circles or recurring typographic treatments may visually connect otherwise very different compositions.

\---

\#\# Gestalt — Continuity

The eye tends to follow continuous paths.

Continuity may be created through:

\- lines  
\- curves  
\- aligned edges  
\- object trajectories  
\- diagonals  
\- repeated shapes  
\- typography  
\- mockup orientation

This can guide attention through a canvas or across multiple canvases.

Use continuity to create movement without necessarily adding explicit arrows.

\---

\#\# Gestalt — Closure

The eye can perceive incomplete shapes as complete.

This allows Creative Lab to use:

\- cropped objects  
\- partial circles  
\- incomplete frames  
\- partially hidden mockups  
\- fragmented typography

without showing everything.

The viewer can reconstruct the whole.

This is one reason aggressive cropping can feel confident rather than incomplete.

Closure explains why a crop can remain understandable, not why it should be used. If the crop weakens product evidence or the relationship to nearby copy, showing the whole object may be stronger.

\---

\#\# Gestalt — Figure and Ground

The viewer distinguishes foreground subjects from background.

This distinction can be:

\- clear  
\- subtle  
\- intentionally ambiguous

Spacing, contrast, color, overlap, and scale influence figure-ground perception.

A mockup may clearly float above the background.

Or it may partially merge into the background as part of the Art Direction.

Both are valid.

\---

\#\# Gestalt — Common Region

Elements placed inside the same visible region tend to feel related.

Examples:

\- card  
\- color field  
\- circular shape  
\- framed area

Use common regions when grouping needs stronger reinforcement than proximity alone.

Avoid wrapping everything in separate containers.

Too many regions fragment the composition.

\---

\#\# Gestalt — Connectedness

Elements connected by lines or shapes appear strongly related.

Useful for:

\- timelines  
\- relationships  
\- feature flows  
\- UI explanations  
\- visual trajectories

Connectedness is powerful.

Use it deliberately, because it can override weaker grouping signals such as proximity.

\---

\#\# Spacing and Typography

Typography depends heavily on spacing.

Consider:

\- line spacing  
\- word spacing  
\- distance between headline and supporting copy  
\- isolation of emphasized words  
\- distance between text and mockup  
\- breathing room around large text

A large headline may need little surrounding decoration because its negative space already contributes to the composition.

Do not compress typography simply to make room for additional assets.

\---

\#\# Spacing and Mockups

The space around a mockup changes its perceived role.

Large isolation can make the device feel iconic.

Tight interaction with typography or shapes can make it feel integrated.

A device touching or leaving the edge can feel larger and more dynamic.

Do not automatically surround mockups with equal margins.

\---

\#\# Spacing and Overlap

Spacing does not always mean separation.

Overlap creates a negative spacing relationship.

It can communicate:

\- connection  
\- depth  
\- hierarchy  
\- energy  
\- integration

Elements may intentionally collide.

The question is whether the collision creates a meaningful visual relationship.

\---

\#\# Margins

Margins influence how constrained or expansive a canvas feels.

Wide margins can feel controlled and refined.

Tight edge relationships can feel bold and energetic.

Margins do not need to be identical on every side.

Asymmetrical margins can contribute to movement and balance.

Choose uniform or varied margins for their optical effect and intended relationships. Uniform margins are legitimate when they create the strongest structure; irregularity is not inherently more creative.

\---

\#\# Edge Tension

An element placed close to an edge creates tension.

An element leaving the canvas increases that effect.

This can be useful for:

\- oversized mockups  
\- typography  
\- illustrations  
\- photos  
\- decorative shapes

Unintentional near-edge placement can look like a mistake.

Intentional edge tension should feel decisive.

If an element is meant to approach the edge, consider whether going further or restoring breathing room creates a stronger result. More tension is useful only when the intended relationship benefits.

\---

\#\# Optical Spacing

Equal numeric distances do not always appear equal.

Different visual shapes require optical adjustment.

For example:

a round icon

and

a rectangular text block

may need different geometric spacing to feel visually aligned.

Judge the rendered result, not only numeric values.

\---

\#\# Repetition of Spacing

Repeated spacing patterns can create rhythm.

For example:

tight  
tight  
large break  
tight

can establish grouping.

Consistency is useful when it clarifies structure.

But perfect repetition can also feel mechanical.

Creative Lab may intentionally vary spacing to create emphasis.

\---

\#\# Spacing Across Multiple Canvases

The screenshot set can have a shared spatial character.

Examples:

\- consistently generous negative space  
\- consistently dense compositions  
\- recurring tight typography  
\- large isolated focal objects  
\- deliberate alternation between dense and open canvases

Consistency does not require identical margins or placement.

It can come from a shared attitude toward space.

\---

\#\# Breaking Spacing Expectations

Creative Lab may intentionally use:

\- extremely tight relationships  
\- oversized gaps  
\- collisions  
\- off-canvas placement  
\- uneven spacing  
\- compressed clusters  
\- isolated tiny elements

These choices can create:

\- tension  
\- humor  
\- boldness  
\- editorial character  
\- maximalism  
\- surprise

Understand the visual consequence before using the exception.

\---

\#\# Shipper Constraints

Spacing decisions should be achievable with available positioning, sizing, grouping, and asset capabilities in Shipper.

If advanced layout behavior is unavailable, preserve the intended relationship using:

\- manual position  
\- scale  
\- overlap  
\- grouping  
\- alignment  
\- crop  
\- canvas edges

Spacing does not require complex rendering technology.

Strong spatial decisions can be created with simple primitives.

\---

\#\# What to Observe

Before execution, inspect:

\- conceptual groups  
\- desired separation  
\- negative space  
\- dense regions  
\- sparse regions  
\- edge relationships  
\- internal spacing  
\- external spacing  
\- repeated spacing patterns  
\- alignment  
\- overlaps  
\- possible Gestalt relationships

After rendering, inspect the whole composition rather than individual numeric gaps.

\---

\#\# Decision Logic

Do not ask:

"Is there enough padding?"

Ask:

"Does this spacing communicate the intended relationship?"

Do not ask:

"Should these elements be closer?"

Ask:

"Should the viewer perceive them as one group?"

Do not ask:

"Is there too much empty space?"

Ask:

"What role is this negative space playing?"

Do not ask:

"Are the margins consistent?"

Ask:

"Does the edge relationship support the Art Direction?"

\---

\#\# Trade-offs

Tight spacing creates connection but may increase density.

Large spacing creates clarity but may weaken relationships.

Uniform spacing creates structure but can feel mechanical.

Irregular spacing creates energy but can appear accidental.

Generous negative space creates confidence but reduces available content area.

Dense grouping creates impact but requires stronger hierarchy.

No trade-off is universally correct.

\---

\#\# Anti-Patterns

Avoid defaulting to:

\#\#\# Filling empty space

Empty space may be one of the strongest elements in the design.

\#\#\# Equal spacing everywhere

Different relationships require different spatial behavior.

\#\#\# Containers for every group

Proximity may be enough.

\#\#\# Uniform margins around every element

Edges can be compositional tools.

\#\#\# Accidental almost-touching

Either create clear separation or intentional interaction.

\#\#\# Dense means bad

Density can be deliberate and expressive.

\#\#\# More space means premium

Premium is an Art Direction, not a spacing formula.

\---

\#\# Visual Critique

After rendering, ask:

1\. Which elements feel grouped?  
2\. Is that grouping intentional?  
3\. Which elements feel separated?  
4\. Does negative space have a clear role?  
5\. Are there accidental gaps?  
6\. Are there accidental near-collisions?  
7\. Does the canvas feel too evenly distributed?  
8\. Could stronger density contrast improve the composition?  
9\. Is a container doing work that spacing could do better?  
10\. Do the margins feel deliberate?  
11\. Is the amount of edge tension appropriate, or would more breathing room improve the relationship?  
12\. Does any empty region need to remain empty?  
13\. Are visual groups easy to perceive?  
14\. Are Gestalt relationships supporting the intended reading?  
15\. Does the set have a coherent attitude toward space?

If spacing feels wrong, identify the relationship that is failing before changing arbitrary numeric values.

\---

\#\# Principle to Remember

Spacing is not empty area.

Spacing is the deliberate control of relationships.

Creative Lab should understand space well enough to group, isolate, compress, separate, connect, or create tension according to the Art Direction.

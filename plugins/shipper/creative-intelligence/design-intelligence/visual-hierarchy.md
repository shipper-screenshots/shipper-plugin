\# Design Intelligence — Visual Hierarchy

\#\# Purpose

Visual hierarchy determines how attention moves through a composition.

Every design creates an order of perception, whether intentional or accidental.

The objective is not to follow a fixed hierarchy such as:

headline → mockup → supporting element

The objective is to understand what deserves attention, decide what should be perceived first, second, and later, and shape the composition accordingly.

Visual hierarchy informs creative decisions. It does not prescribe a universal layout.

\---

\#\# Core Principle

Every composition should have an intentional attention structure.

Before designing, identify:

\- What should attract attention first?  
\- What should be understood second?  
\- What can remain secondary?  
\- What should only be noticed after closer inspection?

Then verify after rendering whether the actual visual result matches that intention.

Do not assume that the most important semantic element automatically becomes the strongest visual element.

Visual importance must be created.

\---

\#\# Sources of Visual Dominance

An element can become dominant through one or several factors:

\- scale  
\- contrast  
\- position  
\- color  
\- saturation  
\- brightness  
\- isolation  
\- surrounding negative space  
\- typography weight  
\- density  
\- complexity  
\- depth  
\- overlap  
\- perspective  
\- rotation  
\- cropping  
\- repetition  
\- visual novelty  
\- recognizable human or emotional content

These factors interact.

A smaller element with extreme contrast can dominate a much larger element.

A large mockup can become visually secondary if it blends into its surroundings.

A small face can attract more attention than a large abstract shape.

Do not evaluate visual weight from size alone.

\---

\#\# Focal Point

A focal point is the element or area that attracts attention first.

A composition can have:

\- one strong focal point  
\- one dominant focal point with several secondary anchors  
\- several deliberately competing focal points  
\- distributed attention when the Art Direction requires it

A single focal point is often useful but is not mandatory.

Controlled competition can create energy.

Uncontrolled competition creates confusion.

Ask:

Why should the eye enter here?

What visual mechanism makes it enter here?

Where does it move next?

\---

\#\# Attention Path

Hierarchy is not only about the strongest element.

It is also about the path between elements.

Consider:

\- entry point  
\- secondary anchor  
\- supporting information  
\- exit point  
\- movement between them

The eye may move vertically, diagonally, circularly, through repetition, through perspective, through faces, or through directional elements.

A composition can deliberately interrupt a predictable reading path to create surprise.

Do not force a conventional top-to-bottom reading order when the Art Direction benefits from another structure.

\---

\#\# Text and Image Relationship

Text and visual content can have different hierarchical relationships.

Possible relationships include:

\#\#\# Text-dominant

Typography is the primary visual object.

The mockup, screenshot, illustration, or image supports it.

\#\#\# Product-dominant

The product UI or visual demonstration carries the message.

Text acts as orientation or reinforcement.

\#\#\# Integrated

Text and visual elements participate in one shared composition.

Neither behaves purely as a caption.

\#\#\# Alternating

Different canvases within the same set may intentionally change dominance.

One canvas may be typography-led.

Another may be UI-led.

Another may be image-led.

Do not make every canvas use the same hierarchy merely for consistency.

Consistency should come from Art Direction, not layout repetition.

\---

\#\# Typography as Hierarchy

Typography creates hierarchy through:

\- size  
\- weight  
\- width  
\- case  
\- contrast  
\- line breaking  
\- position  
\- spacing  
\- alignment  
\- isolation  
\- typeface personality

A headline does not need to be the largest element.

A supporting word may intentionally become oversized.

A single word can dominate the composition while the rest of the sentence remains secondary.

Line breaks may be used to create hierarchy rather than simply accommodate width.

Examples of intentional hierarchy:

"Make  
NEW  
friends"

"Trade Right  
Where You  
CHAT"

"75  
DAYS"

The typography may become part of the visual concept.

Do not treat text only as information placed above a mockup.

\---

\#\# Scale Contrast

Clear hierarchy requires perceptual differentiation, but that differentiation does not need to come from dramatic scale.

Scale is one lever among contrast, position, isolation, color, weight, spacing, and depth.

Moderate scale relationships can be equally effective when the intended Art Direction is restrained or product-led.

When two elements are intended to have different importance but appear similarly weighted, consider increasing the contrast between them rather than making minor adjustments.

This can mean:

\- making one dramatically larger  
\- making the other deliberately smaller  
\- cropping the dominant element  
\- isolating the secondary element  
\- reducing contrast on supporting content

Subtlety is valid when intentional.

Do not default to subtle hierarchy when the Art Direction requires impact.

\---

\#\# Contrast

Contrast can be created through:

\- light vs dark  
\- saturated vs muted  
\- large vs small  
\- detailed vs simple  
\- sharp vs soft  
\- dense vs empty  
\- geometric vs organic  
\- static vs dynamic  
\- horizontal vs diagonal  
\- flat vs dimensional  
\- restrained vs expressive

Hierarchy can therefore be created without changing size.

Use the contrast type that supports the intended visual language.

\---

\#\# Position

Position affects perceived importance.

Elements near visual centers, edges, corners, or intersections can behave very differently.

Do not assume centered means important.

An isolated object near an edge can become extremely dominant.

An oversized object partially leaving the canvas can feel stronger than the same object centered and fully visible.

Stronger is not automatically more appropriate. A centered, fully visible object may give the intended message the clearest attention structure. Choose the relationship, not the most dramatic position.

Use position deliberately.

\---

\#\# Isolation

Negative space can increase importance.

An element surrounded by empty space often attracts more attention than an element surrounded by many competitors.

Isolation can be stronger than scale.

Do not fill empty areas automatically.

Empty space may exist specifically to make another element stronger.

\---

\#\# Overlap and Depth

Overlapping elements establish relationships and depth.

An element placed in front of another can gain dominance.

An element partially hidden can create curiosity.

Foreground elements can direct attention toward background content.

Depth may be created through:

\- overlap  
\- scale  
\- blur  
\- perspective  
\- shadows  
\- transparency  
\- cropping  
\- foreground objects

Use depth when it serves the Art Direction.

Do not add depth only to make a design appear more sophisticated.

\---

\#\# Cropping

Cropping changes hierarchy.

A partially visible object can feel larger, closer, and more energetic than the same object fully contained within the canvas.

This is especially useful with:

\- device mockups  
\- screenshots  
\- illustrations  
\- typography  
\- photography  
\- decorative shapes

A crop can communicate confidence.

It tells the viewer that the full object does not need to be shown to be understood.

Neither fitting nor cropping every important object is a creative default. Show as much as the intended hierarchy and product meaning benefit from; a crop that steals attention from the message weakens that hierarchy.

\---

\#\# Human Attention

Faces, eyes, hands, familiar objects, and emotionally expressive imagery can strongly attract attention.

When using them, account for their natural visual dominance.

A human face can unintentionally overpower:

\- the product  
\- the headline  
\- the intended feature  
\- a CTA-like element

This is not necessarily a problem.

It becomes a problem when the actual hierarchy contradicts the intended message.

\---

\#\# Decorative Elements

Decorative elements can support hierarchy by:

\- directing attention  
\- framing the focal point  
\- creating depth  
\- creating rhythm  
\- connecting areas  
\- reinforcing Art Direction

They should not compete with the main message accidentally.

Decoration may intentionally become prominent when it is part of the concept.

The question is not:

"Is this decorative?"

The question is:

"What role does this element play in the hierarchy?"

\---

\#\# Hierarchy Across a Screenshot Set

Hierarchy should also be considered across multiple canvases.

The entire set does not need identical hierarchy.

Variation can create rhythm and keep attention alive.

Possible sequence:

Canvas 1:  
Typography dominant.

Canvas 2:  
Mockup dominant.

Canvas 3:  
UI detail dominant.

Canvas 4:  
Human or illustration dominant.

Canvas 5:  
Typography dominant again.

This creates controlled variation while preserving a coherent Art Direction.

Avoid reproducing:

headline  
\+  
mockup  
\+  
small decoration

with identical visual weight across every canvas unless repetition is itself an intentional Art Direction choice.

\---

\#\# Breaking Hierarchy Intentionally

Not every composition needs immediate clarity.

Ambiguity, density, collision, fragmentation, or competing elements can be used intentionally.

This may create:

\- energy  
\- tension  
\- playfulness  
\- editorial character  
\- youthfulness  
\- visual surprise

Breaking conventional hierarchy is valid when:

\- the result remains intentional  
\- the message is still recoverable  
\- the visual experience supports the Art Direction

Do not confuse unconventional hierarchy with accidental hierarchy.

\---

\#\# What to Observe

Before execution, inspect:

\- intended focal point  
\- relative visual weight of every major element  
\- attention path  
\- competing elements  
\- empty areas  
\- dominant color regions  
\- dominant text  
\- dominant imagery  
\- scale relationships  
\- overlap  
\- depth  
\- edge interactions  
\- crop behavior

After rendering, observe the image as pixels rather than as a list of assets.

The intended structure may not survive execution.

\---

\#\# Decision Logic

Do not ask:

"Is the headline large enough?"

Ask:

"What currently attracts attention first, and is that what I intended?"

Do not ask:

"Should the mockup be centered?"

Ask:

"What position creates the relationship I want between the mockup and the rest of the composition?"

Do not ask:

"Is there too much empty space?"

Ask:

"What function is this empty space performing?"

Do not ask:

"Should this element be smaller?"

Ask:

"Is its visual weight appropriate to its role?"

\---

\#\# Trade-offs

Strong hierarchy can improve immediate comprehension but may reduce subtlety.

Distributed hierarchy can create richness but may require more viewing time.

Large scale can create impact but reduce visible product detail.

Dense compositions can feel energetic but make prioritization harder.

Minimal compositions can create confidence but become generic when decisions lack specificity; expressive compositions have the same risk.

No trade-off has one universally correct answer.

Choose based on the intended Art Direction and the role of the canvas.

\---

\#\# Anti-Patterns

Avoid defaulting to:

\#\#\# Everything has similar visual weight

This produces weak hierarchy.

\#\#\# Headline always first

The product, image, or visual concept may deserve dominance.

\#\#\# Mockup always largest

A device is an asset, not automatically the hero.

\#\#\# Everything centered

Centering without intention often removes tension and character.

Centering with intention can create clarity or iconic presence. Asymmetry without intention is no improvement.

\#\#\# Empty space must be filled

Negative space may be doing important compositional work.

\#\#\# Small decorative elements everywhere

Decoration without hierarchy creates noise.

\#\#\# Every canvas uses the same hierarchy

Consistency does not require repetition.

\#\#\# Tiny hierarchy adjustments

If the relationship between two elements is unclear, a stronger contrast may be more effective than repeated minor resizing.

\---

\#\# Visual Critique

After rendering, evaluate the actual result.

Ask:

1\. Where does my eye go first?  
2\. Is that intentional?  
3\. What do I notice second?  
4\. Is the reading path natural or confused?  
5\. Are two elements competing unintentionally?  
6\. Is an intended secondary element too dominant?  
7\. Does the mockup overpower the message?  
8\. Does the headline overpower the product unnecessarily?  
9\. Does an illustration or human figure steal attention?  
10\. Is negative space strengthening hierarchy or simply accidental?  
11\. Are decorative elements supporting attention or fragmenting it?  
12\. Does the hierarchy support the intended Art Direction, whether quiet or emphatic?  
13\. Would increasing or reducing scale, crop, contrast, or overlap improve the intended attention path?  
14\. Does this canvas feel distinct from the others while remaining coherent with the set?

If hierarchy fails, identify the cause before modifying assets.

Do not automatically increase the headline size.

Determine which visual relationship is wrong and change the most relevant variable.

\---

\#\# Principle to Remember

Hierarchy is not a template.

Hierarchy is the intentional control of attention.

Creative Lab should know where attention is likely to go, understand why, and remain free to direct, distribute, delay, or disrupt that attention when the Art Direction benefits from it.
